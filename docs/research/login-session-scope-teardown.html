§1 — The symptom, and why it resisted diagnosis

Three times over two days the entire agent fleet on the login node 98dci4-srv-1006 ended simultaneously: eighteen sessions at 19:56:22 and again at 22:36:23 on 2026-09-18, and nine processes at 02:31:11 on 2026-09-20. Each time the multiplexer sessions were left behind in a state that looked survivable, the agents inside them were gone, and nothing in the kernel log, the scheduler or the memory counters explained it.

The first two were reconstructed hours afterwards from file modification times, which is why they produced three plausible and wrong hypotheses in succession. The third was recorded as it happened by two purpose-built instruments, which named the signal, its delivery mechanism and every victim in one reading, and narrowed the remaining unknown to a single question. The difference between those two experiences is the main engineering lesson here and is recorded in §6.

§2 — What the instruments recorded

A signal canary — a shell that traps every catchable signal, logs which one arrived, and deliberately does not die — was running in three multiplexer panes: one in the live nova session and two in a session with no client attached at all. At the moment of the event all three logged the same pair:

02:31:11.782  marker(3559642) received SIGTERM      <- unattached session
02:31:11.782  marker(3559640) received SIGTERM      <- unattached session
02:31:11.783  marker(293705)  received SIGTERM      <- attached session
02:31:12.785  marker(3559642) received SIGHUP
02:31:12.785  marker(3559640) received SIGHUP
02:31:12.785  marker(293705)  received SIGHUP

SIGTERM, then SIGHUP 1.003 seconds later, to all three within one millisecond of each other. Two of those panes were in a session nobody was looking at, which already rules out a terminal hangup reaching a foreground job.

A second instrument sampling process topology every five seconds recorded what actually died: nine processes, comprising three agent workers that were fully detached (parent 1, own session id, no controlling terminal), two agents inside multiplexer panes, two multiplexer servers, two multiplexer clients — and the recorder itself, which caught the same SIGTERM through its own handler and wrote recorder stopped before exiting.

Processes with no controlling terminal died. That is the observation that eliminates every terminal-based explanation at once.

The sampler records that a process vanished, never why, so not all nine are attributable to the signal. The canary is the instrument that proves signalling, and it covered two scopes. Of the nine, the two multiplexer servers, the agents in their panes, the detached workers and the recorder were all inside those two scopes. One further agent sat in a pane of a multiplexer server that survived, and since a server demonstrably does not survive this signal (§4a), its scope cannot have been signalled — so that one disappearance is a coincidence of timing and is left unattributed rather than folded into the count.

The session looks like it survived. It did not.

The most confusing part of the symptom is that the multiplexer session comes back afterwards with its tabs and layout intact, showing a pane that reads Waiting to run: claude --resume with <ENTER> run beneath it. That reads as though the session lived and only the process inside it was killed, which is the wrong way round and sent this investigation down two blind alleys.

It is resurrection, not survival. This account runs with session_serialization true, serialize_pane_viewport true and scrollback_lines_to_serialize 50000, so the multiplexer continuously writes each session's layout and scrollback to disk. When the server is killed the session is gone; the next attach rebuilds it from that file and offers to re-run each pane's recorded command rather than restarting it. The restored layout is a photograph, not a survivor — which is also why the work inside is not resumed and why the pane waits for a keypress.

§3 — What it is not, each excluded by a measurement

HypothesisThe measurement that excludes it
Memory exhaustion / out-of-memory kill memory.current 6.38 GB of 64 GB and pids.current 101 at the instant of the kill; oom_kill 529 byte-identical before and after. The node was running at low concurrency.
CPU-quota throttling killing processes Throttling stalls a process, it never signals one. Real and serious in its own right, but not a killer.
Terminal hangup propagating to pane children Two of the three canaries were in a session with no client, and three of the dead workers had tty=?.
A site reaper, cron job or idle-session killer No entry in /etc/cron.d, no arbiter, no logind idle action, and no system timer anywhere near the event. The signal lands 0.78 s after the final logout, not on a schedule.
Scheduler epilog cleaning up after a job Epilog = (null); the login node carries no allocation.
A client detaching Tested directly. A detached marker was started inside a dedicated login session, then that session's client was killed outright. The marker received no signal and was still running afterwards; the scope went to SubState=abandoned, which is precisely what KillUserProcesses=no promises.

That last row matters most, because it is the intuitive answer and it is wrong. One connection dropping — a closed laptop, a dead VPN, a killed terminal — abandons its scope and leaves everything inside it running.

§4 — The mechanism

A multiplexer server does not survive this signal

Measured directly, because the whole account turns on it. A throwaway session was created with one pane, then SIGTERM was sent to the server and the pane process together, as a control-group signal would deliver it. Both died and the session disappeared from the listing. So a server that is still running after one of these events was never signalled — its scope was not among those stopped — and server survival is a reliable read on which scopes were hit.

Every login session gets a systemd scope, and on this host those scopes are configured:

session-*.scope     KillSignal=15 (SIGTERM)   SendSIGHUP=yes   KillMode=control-group
user@39486.service  ActiveEnterTimestamp = Sun 2026-09-20 02:31:12 CEST

KillSignal and SendSIGHUP together produce exactly the SIGTERM-then-SIGHUP pair the canary caught. KillMode=control-group is why it reached everything: the signal goes to every process in the scope's control group, not to a foreground process group and not down a terminal. And the user manager re-entered active at 02:31:12 — the same second as the SIGHUP — because lingering is enabled and it was restarted immediately after being stopped.

The blast radius is one scope, and a scope is wider than it looks. A multiplexed SSH connection carries several terminals — the sshd process at the time of the event was serving pts/1, pts/15 and pts/18 — and puts all of them, plus everything ever launched through any of them, in a single control group. Two scopes were signalled at 02:31:11, and between them they held both multiplexer servers that died, the agents in their panes, the detached workers and the recorder.

The sender, from the system journal

Privileged journal access settled this, and it is systemd itself performing a complete teardown of the user — not a session scope ending on its own, and not third-party software. The user manager logs the whole sequence:

systemd[2817083]: Activating special unit Exit the Session...
systemd[2817083]: Stopping reckon plan server...
systemd[2817083]: Stopping IMAS Codex LiteLLM Proxy...
systemd[2817083]: imas-codex-llm.service: Main process exited, code=exited, status=143/n/a
systemd[2817083]: Removed slice User Core Session Slice.
systemd[2817083]: Removed slice User Application Slice.
systemd[2817083]: Reached target Exit the Session.
systemd[1]:      user@39486.service: Deactivated successfully.
systemd[1]:      Starting User Manager for UID 39486...        <- 1-2 seconds later

Two units are being stopped, not one, which is what the one-second gap between the SIGTERM and the SIGHUP was telling us. The live session scopes are torn down, taking every process started through those connections; and separately the user manager is stopped, which removes app.slice and session.slice and kills the services inside them — status=143 is SIGTERM, the same 143 that appears in the terminal. Lingering then restarts the user manager a second or two later, which is why its start timestamp always sits just after the event.

It repeats often. The same teardown appears twice in a five-hour window on 2026-09-18, at 00:57:08 and at 05:46:41.

What remains unexplained is why it happens at all, because lingering is enabled for this account and persisted on disk (/var/lib/systemd/linger/mcintos, written 2026-08-08). Lingering exists precisely to keep a user manager running when the user has no sessions, and it is not holding. That is now a narrow, answerable question to put to the site with the journal lines above as evidence, rather than the open-ended one it replaced.

Earlier drafts of this record reasoned from user-space observation alone. Four of this account's scopes currently read SubState=abandoned, which is what KillUserProcesses=no produces when a session ends; two of them still hold multiplexer servers that have run for two days. So an abandoned scope is plainly survivable, and the controlled test in §3 reproduced that abandonment with no signal at all. Something stopped the two scopes that died while leaving the two older abandoned ones alone, and the difference between them is not visible from user space. The system journal would name the caller; it is readable only by root on this host, so closing that last gap needs either elevated access or a question to the site.

That gap does not change the remedy. Whatever issues the stop, it addresses a control group, so what decides a process's fate is which control group it was born in — and that is entirely within our control.

Which branch of the control-group tree a process sat in decided whether it lived:

user-39486.slice
├─ session-136289.scope   opened Sep 18 13:27, still active   ambix multiplexer server    SURVIVED
├─ session-136452.scope   opened Sep 18 14:36, still active   reckon multiplexer server   SURVIVED
├─ session-138854.scope   tied to the terminals that closed   nova server + its agents    KILLED
├─ session-138964.scope   tied to the terminals that closed   second server + its agents  KILLED
└─ user@39486.service
   └─ app.slice
      └─ session-loss-recorder.service      <- placed here after the event; outside every scope

The survivors were not more robust. They were simply born in older scopes that happened to still be open.

Nothing changed on the host

The natural suspicion is a recent change to the login node, and the dates rule it out. systemd is 252-14.el9_2.3, installed 2 April 2025; logind.conf has not been touched since 17 July 2023; the slice drop-in carrying the resource caps was last written 21 March 2026 and sets no kill policy; and the node has been up since 8 August 2026 with no reboot. The behaviour is not new.

Nor is it load. An earlier draft of this record attributed the change to our own scaling — thirty concurrent sessions, multiplexed connections, workers outliving their connection. That is a real description of the CPU-quota problem and it does not apply here: fewer than five agent processes were running when the 02:31 event fired, and the memory and task counters confirm it. Concurrency is irrelevant to this failure and the claim is withdrawn.

So neither the host nor our load explains the timing, which leaves the trigger genuinely unidentified. What has been excluded by inspection rather than by argument: no pam_exec hook on session close anywhere in /etc/pam.d; no cleanup script under /usr/local, /opt/*/bin or /etc/profile.d that signals user processes; a root crontab carrying only a configuration-management scan on Sunday at 01:07 and an upload at reboot; no sshd client-alive setting; and nothing in the kernel ring buffer for the window. Three privileged agents do run as root on the node — an endpoint-protection service, a log forwarder and a metrics agent — and the first of those is the class of thing that can terminate a process. There is no evidence that it did, and it is named here only because it was wrong to exclude third-party software as confidently as an earlier draft did.

§5 — Why everything that looked detached was not

setsid, nohup, and a subprocess launched with start_new_session=True all detach a process from its controlling terminal and give it a new POSIX session. None of them move it out of the systemd control group it was started in. A worker can have parent 1, its own session id and no terminal, and still be sitting in the login scope of the SSH connection that spawned its grandparent — which is exactly what the recorder captured.

This is why the crew dispatcher's start_new_session=True, which is correct and worth keeping, bought nothing here; why the multiplexer's whole purpose — outliving the client — did not protect the servers; and why a recorder started with setsid nohup was killed alongside what it was watching. Detachment in the process sense and detachment in the cgroup sense are different properties, and only the second one was ever relevant.

§6 — The fix, and the instrumentation lesson

A long-lived process must not be born in a login session scope. Two placements achieve that, and they are not equivalent.

A transient user unit does not work, and an earlier draft of this record recommended it. Placing a process at user@39486.service/app.slice/<name>.service does move it out of the caller's session scope, which is real but insufficient: the journal shows Removed slice User Application Slice in the same teardown, and the two long-running services that live there are killed with status=143 every time. The placement escapes one of the two units being stopped and not the other. A unit with Restart=always returns afterwards, which is adequate for a daemon and useless for a multiplexer server holding live session state, because the process it was protecting is gone either way.

So the only placement that survives is outside user-39486.slice altogether.

That leaves a scheduler allocation. A job's control group is owned by the node daemon and sits entirely outside the login manager's session lifecycle, so closing a terminal — or closing all of them — cannot reach it. That is the placement the fleet-hosting plan already adopts, and this diagnosis is independent evidence for it: the failure being escaped is not resource contention but session lifetime.

The instrument that was missing

Three rounds of signal logging narrowed the sender to "something with permission" and then stopped, because a trap handler records which signal arrived and cannot record who sent it. Blocking the signals and collecting them with sigwaitinfo() returns the kernel's siginfo instead, which carries the sending process id and uid, and whose si_code separates a deliberate kill() from a signal the kernel raised. A probe doing this is armed in three different control groups — a pane of a session that has been hit before, a pane of one that came through untouched, and a user unit that the placement fix says should never be signalled at all. The next occurrence names the sender instead of describing it.

A faster route exists and costs one request: the journal on that node is readable only by adm, systemd-journal or wheel, verified rather than assumed. Either adding the account to one of those groups, or asking the site to read the logind journal for 02:31:11 on 2026-09-20, would answer today what the probe answers at the next failure.

What is in place, and what it does not cover

The teardown fires when the account's session count reaches zero. Every session normally originates from one workstation, so they end together whenever it sleeps or loses the network, and the count reaches zero every time. A session anchored somewhere that never sleeps removes that precondition without needing the linger defect fixed first.

A one-CPU scheduler job on an idle partition holds an SSH session to the login node and reconnects on a ten-second loop if it drops. It runs inside the job's control group, owned by the node daemon, so the teardown it prevents cannot reach the thing preventing it. With it running the count falls to one rather than to zero, and loginctl shows an active session whose remote host is a compute node rather than the workstation.

Three limits, stated because a prop that is believed to be a cure is worse than no prop. It does not repair lingering, so the defect remains and the site request stands. It fails silently if the job ends — a cancellation is not requeued, though a node failure is — so the job's presence is now something worth checking whenever sessions die again. And it defends the user slice rather than leaving it, which is what the allocation in the fleet-hosting plan does instead; that remains the durable answer.

A tunnel from the workstation is not a substitute. Those connections are independent of the terminal multiplexer's shared master and do hold their own sessions, so they narrow the window during a terminal-only dropout — but they originate from the same machine, so they die with it, which is exactly when the count reaches zero.

Why it surfaced when it did — resolved

The teardown stops live session scopes and leaves abandoned ones alone. A scope is abandoned once the connection that created it closes, so a multiplexer server is vulnerable only while the connection it was born in is still open. After that it is permanently safe, because nothing will stop a scope logind has already released.

That is why the same teardown is sometimes harmless and sometimes total. Servers that have been running for a day or more were born in connections long since closed; their scopes read abandoned and the teardown cannot reach them. Measured on three live servers, every one of them sits in an abandoned scope, and every one has survived multiple teardowns.

The transcripts make the change visible. Across the two teardowns on the morning of 2026-09-18, 27 agent sessions wrote both before and after — they were untouched, with three ending near the first and none near the second. At the teardown two days later only three survived. The mechanism did not change between those dates; the placement of every multiplexer server did.

A multiplexer upgrade at 07:05 that morning is what moved them. Replacing the binary forces every server to restart, so a set of servers that had drifted into abandoned scopes over days was recreated all at once inside the connections that were open at that moment. From then until those connections closed, every one of them was exposed, and the first teardown that arrived — that evening — took the lot. The upgrade is not a defect and the multiplexer's own log shows a clean signalled shutdown with no panic; what it did was relocate every long-running server from a safe control group into a vulnerable one simultaneously.

The exposure is therefore self-healing and recurring: a new session is at risk for as long as the connection that created it stays open, and safe afterwards. The dangerous pattern is the ordinary one — start a session, leave the connection open, and let the machine sleep.

Supporting evidence for the timing

Neither the mechanism nor its precondition is new. The system software predates the failures by more than a year, and the login record shows the precondition occurring routinely: counting moments where three or more terminals ended in the same second gives 114 events across 29 days, on every working day since the record begins. What did change is the size of those events, from three or four terminals ending together through early September to five and six from the middle of the month. More terminals riding one connection means a single drop takes a larger share of the account's sessions.

That is suggestive and it is not proof, so it is recorded as such. One query separates the two candidate explanations, and it needs privileged journal access:

journalctl -u user@<uid>.service --since "2026-08-10" | grep -c "Exit the Session"
journalctl -u user@<uid>.service --since "2026-08-10" | grep    "Exit the Session"

A teardown count that is high and evenly spread over weeks means the trigger did not change and what changed is what the teardown now destroys, because long-lived agent work on that node is recent. A count that begins abruptly in mid-September means the connection topology changed and that is where to look next. Until one of those is observed, the question stays open.

The instrumentation lesson is worth as much as the fix. Two events were reconstructed from modification times and produced three confident wrong answers, because an mtime records that something ended and never what ended it. The third was answered in a single reading by two cheap instruments: a process that traps signals and refuses to die names the sender's intent, and a five-second sampler of process topology names the victims and their control-group placement. Neither is clever. Both had to exist before the event, which is the only hard part.
