Written 2026-10-15 to support the decision on an 8×B300 server, set against the 8×H200 NVL node this project runs on (98dci4-gpu-0003). It covers three workload classes, each with this project's own measurements where they exist. Serving is summarised from the companion study, serving capacity and the 8×B300 projection, which holds the detail. Model training and FP64 scientific computing are assessed here. The earlier accelerator study covers cooling, form factor and price.

§1 — The answer in brief

B300 is a very fast card for low-precision work and a very slow one for FP64. Per GPU it has about 2.7 times our H200 NVL card's BF16, FP8 and TF32 throughput, 1.7 times its memory bandwidth and twice its memory, and it adds FP4. It has one-fiftieth of its FP64 tensor throughput and one-eleventh of its INT8. NVIDIA built Blackwell Ultra for inference and low-precision training, and the FP64 cut is deliberate: 1.2 TFLOP/s against 37 on the B200 of the same generation.

The recommendation follows from the table. Buy B300 for serving and for model training. Keep FP64 physics on the H200 node, or move it to tiered precision before it moves to B300. Before ordering, run three benchmarks this repository already has on a trial B300: the serve census, one training step of the world model, and scripts/fsa_gpu_capability.py in FP64 (§6).

The INT8 cut has a second consequence. NVIDIA's recent answer to weak FP64 is emulation: running double-precision matrix products on integer tensor cores (the Ozaki scheme, offered in recent cuBLAS). On B300 the INT8 units were cut as well, so emulated FP64 GEMM is not a way back on this card. It is on B200.

§4 — Model training

What this project trains. The models here are small by LLM standards, and they train in BF16 on one node. They include a 125M-parameter transformer (imas_ambix/train/configs/v0-125m.yaml), video and signal tokenizers and their decoders (imas_ambix/tokenizer/), the camera-dynamics and machine-agnostic world models (imas_ambix/worldmodel/), and the GS-grounded latent engine. Two recorded figures give their scale on H200. The latent engine trained at about 17 steps a second across six ranks with data-parallel all-reduce, 51,456 training pairs (job 1224777). The in-process tokenizer encoder sustained 308 frames a second per GPU, and 4.02 billion tokens in about seven hours on one card.

Performance: what to expect, and why it is less than the datasheet ratio for our models. The tensor-throughput ratio is 2.7 at BF16 and FP8. A large transformer reaches most of it, because its matrix products dominate. Our models are small. At 100M to 1B parameters the step is shared with data loading from GPFS, with kernel-launch latency and with optimizer and normalisation kernels that are bandwidth-bound and gain only 1.7 times. Data loading gains nothing at all. So expect 1.5 to 2.5 times realised per GPU on today's models at today's batch sizes. More is available by raising the batch into the extra memory. This is an estimate, not a measurement; §6 names the step benchmark that would settle it.

Public training measurements, read with care. In MLPerf Training v5.1 (November 2025), NVIDIA reported Blackwell Ultra at more than four times Hopper on Llama 3.1 405B pretraining with the same GPU count. Those submissions used FP4 recipes, against FP8 for Hopper, so part of the factor is the change of format. Lambda's independent GB300 NVL72 entry was 1.27 times GB200 NVL72 on Llama 2 70B LoRA fine-tuning. Both are rack-scale NVL72 systems, which run faster per GPU than an 8-GPU HGX board: by 12 to 14% in vLLM's DeepSeek measurement, and by up to about two times at some of InferenceX's serving points. For an 8×B300 node against 8×H200 on large-model training, 2.3 to 2.7 times at matched precision is the defensible figure, rising toward 4 with FP4.

§5 — Scientific computing in FP64

This project depends on FP64. Four modules switch JAX to double precision: the contour-free flux-surface average (imas_ambix/latent/flux_surface_connectivity.py), null finding (stencil_nulls.py), the boundary classifier (connectivity_boundary.py) and the NOVA ensemble estimator (imas_ambix/statespace/nova_ensemble_estimator.py). The equilibrium solve runs in FP64 on the host. The H200 runs the flux-surface average in FP64 at about 194,000 equilibria a second at batch 1,024, matching the CPU to 1.1e-13 (imas_ambix/spine_bench/results/fsa-gpu-capability-2e35d090c7-98dci4-gpu-0003.yaml).

The roofline puts B300 behind for almost all FP64 work. A kernel doing less than 0.15 floating-point operations per byte it moves is limited by memory on both cards, and B300 runs it 1.7 times faster. Above that intensity B300 is capped at 1.2 TFLOP/s. A stencil or contour kernel typically sits between 0.1 and 1 flop per byte, which is already at or past B300's ridge. Its FP64 GEMMs and Newton-Jacobian products sit at 10 to 100, where H200 reaches 60 TFLOP/s and B300 stays at 1.2, fifty times slower. This study has not measured where each of our FP64 kernels sits. The flux-surface average's 194,000 equilibria a second could fall several-fold on B300, or could hold, depending on its intensity. That is why the FP64 benchmark in §6 matters more than any other.

The tiered design is what makes physics portable. The GPU labeler's lead decision of 2026-07-20 runs the Green's-function matrix product in BF16 or TF32 with FP32 accumulation, topology in FP32, and only the recurrent state in FP64. Its spike measured 259 and 202 TFLOP/s on H200 for the matrix product, in its two configurations at batch 512. The axis positions moved by 0.004 cm at TF32 and 0.019 cm at BF16. On B300 that product gains about 2.6 times. The FP64 remainder is a tridiagonal diffusion step and the passive-circuit recurrence, both small and sequential, so it is limited by latency rather than throughput. Code written this way gains on B300. Code that is FP64 throughout loses, and should stay on H200 until it is tiered.
