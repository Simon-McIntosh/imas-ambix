§2 — What four H200 cards deliver today

The pass time is a straight line, and that decides the projection. A pass costs 20.0 ms plus 4.0 ms for every running request, with a residual of 1.5 ms across widths 1 to 12. The per-request term is not expert traffic. Expert traffic grows ever more slowly as the batch's experts overlap, and this line does not bend. What grows linearly is work each request brings with it: sparse attention over a context of about 125,000 tokens, and the prefill of that request's next turn, which runs between decode steps on the same cards. The 2026-10-08 study measured the share of seconds carrying any prefill rising from 0.17 at one request to 0.70 at twelve. So on these cards, aggregate decode flattens because prefill and long-context attention take the time, not because the KV pool or the experts run out. At width 12 the pool is 36% used.

What a request experiences. Since the gate change, a completed request presents a median of 120,135 prompt tokens and 90th percentile 169,747. 96.8% of presented prompt tokens are served from the prefix cache, so each turn prefills about 3,700 new tokens. The median completion is 376 tokens and the mean 745. Time to first token has a median of 0.74 s and a 90th percentile of 1.56 s. The router's own receipts give a median per-request decode rate of 62 tok/s, with 10th and 90th percentiles of 38 and 106. That median is higher than the engine's figure at width 12 because many requests run at lower widths. Waiting at the router gate is zero for most requests, but its 90th percentile rose from 0.0001 s before the change to 1.8 s after, and its 99th from 6.6 s to 17.5 s. The gate is now doing its job, and some of the time it is the constraint.

The lane is busy in bursts. Over 299 hours the mean is 4.0 running requests and 237 tok/s of output. The median hour has 2.8 running, the 90th-percentile hour 9.6. 41 hours, 14%, had essentially nothing running, and 70 hours averaged at least 400 tok/s. In the router's own record, the share of time with no request in flight is 32%, with 1 to 4 in flight 34%, with 5 to 11 in flight 28%, and with 12 or more in flight 6%. A faster node will be bought for the peaks and for the per-request rate. It will not be bought for utilisation, which is the same conclusion the 2026-09-17 study reached.

The compaction itself has a cost that grows with the serve's age. Each 300 s pass re-reads the whole raw file. §4 — The cost estimate: why the month halved

The ledger reprices the past. watch.period_row prices a whole period once, at price_at(price, now): the provider table it last fetched, at the current time of day. The plan's own §9 says each interval should be priced at the rate in force when it ran; the code does not do that. So whenever OpenRouter's headline moves, every token of the month is repriced the moment the table refreshes. The published month row today carries 57.9 B input and 0.400 B output tokens, and reads $732. The router's receipts agree to within 3%, at 56.4 B and 0.382 B.

The headline moves a lot, and it is not anyone's price. These are the headline rates that can still be recovered for deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash, in dollars per million tokens:

What to change. §5 — 8×B300: what changes for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash

The hardware. A B300 (Blackwell Ultra) on an HGX baseboard carries 288 GB of HBM3e at about 8 TB/s, against 141 GB at 4.8 TB/s on our H200 NVL. NVIDIA's datasheet lists 270 GB usable, about 2.1 TB per node. Dense FP4 is about 13 to 15 PFLOPS per GPU against roughly 2 PFLOPS of FP8 on H200. All eight GPUs share one NVSwitch domain at 1.8 TB/s each, and each GPU has an 800 Gb/s ConnectX-8 NIC in NVIDIA's reference build. Eight such cards against our four give 3.3 times the memory bandwidth, about five times the FP8 compute, about fifteen times that compute at FP4, and four times the memory.

The method. §2's pass time splits into a 20.0 ms fixed term and a 4.0 ms per-request term. On 8×B300 the fixed term, which is expert reads plus collective and launch latency, is divided by ka. The per-request term, which is long-context attention plus the interleaved prefill of each request's next turn, is divided by kb. The low case takes ka 1.7 and kb 3.3. That is the bandwidth ratio at today's 20% efficiency, diluted by an unchanged latency floor, and a per-request term that gains only bandwidth. The high case takes ka 2.5 and kb 5.0. That is the 2026-09-17 study's block-scale tax removed, with prefill gaining a share of the compute ratio. The accept length stays at today's 3.1. vLLM's recipe uses a five-token DSpark block on Blackwell with a reported acceptance of 3.51, which would lift every figure by about 13%.

Memory stops being a constraint. On 8×B300 the 511 GB checkpoint fits whole, Engram tables included, so the host offload we use today is not needed. vLLM's Blackwell recipe runs it as DEP8 with up to 256 sequences. That leaves well over 1 TB for KV. At our measured 10.1 KB per token, one 125,000-token conversation needs about 1.3 GB, so over a thousand conversations would stay resident. The 4M-token pool we run now holds about 32.

Public measurements agree on the scale. SemiAnalysis InferenceX publishes per-GPU throughput for DeepSeek R1 at 8K input and 1K output tokens. At 50 tok/s per user, B300 delivers 5,852 tok/s per GPU against H200's 1,591, a factor of 3.7. At 89 tok/s per user the factor is 2.8, and at 128 it is 1.6. Doubling for eight cards against our four gives a node factor of 7.4, 5.6 and 3.2. Our projection at 45 tok/s per request is a factor of 3.9 to 6.3. That sits slightly below the public figures, for reasons that are expected: their prompts are 8K tokens against our 120K, so per-request attention and prefill are cheaper, and their H200 is the SXM part rather than our 600 W NVL card. vLLM reports a single GB300 in a DGX Station serving this exact model at 416 to 423 tok/s across 16 streams, with its Engram tables in host memory. That is about 80% of our four H200 cards at width 12, from one Blackwell Ultra GPU.

What this study cannot settle. The per-request term's split between attention and prefill is inferred from its linearity and from the 2026-10-08 prefill-active shares, not measured directly. If prefill is the larger part, the high case is closer, because prefill is compute-bound and gains the most from Blackwell. The projection also assumes our router's global gate is lifted for the new node, which §8 makes a precondition.

Our router already serves two models on one host by port.

§7 — Interconnect: inside the box and between boxes

Inside the box, interconnect is decisive, and HGX B300 already has it. Mixture-of-experts serving exchanges activations between GPUs at every layer: tensor-parallel all-reduces, and expert-parallel dispatch and combine. On our H200 NVL cards those exchanges ride bridges that join four cards into a clique, measured at 159 GB/s per direction per pair, with PCIe beyond the clique. That is why the 2026-09-17 study found eight-card runs here losing to four-card ones. HGX B300 puts all eight GPUs on one NVSwitch at 1.8 TB/s each, about four times our cards' per-GPU NVLink and fully connected. Specify the HGX (SXM) baseboard, not PCIe cards, when ordering. A PCIe Blackwell card would repeat today's island boundary.

Between boxes, bandwidth barely matters for a pair of serving nodes. Each serving node holds complete models, and only HTTP requests cross the network. A 125,000-token prompt is about half a megabyte of JSON, so even gigabit Ethernet moves it in a few milliseconds. The fast NICs, 800 Gb/s ConnectX-8 per GPU, matter in three cases:

§8 — How datacentres route requests, and what our router needs

The common shape. An L7 gateway terminates the request, and an endpoint picker chooses a replica. Kubernetes stacks implement it through the Gateway API Inference Extension; llm-d runs a pipeline of filters and weighted scorers there, prefix-cache affinity against queue depth and KV load. NVIDIA Dynamo's KV router keeps a global prefix tree built from the KV-block events workers report. It routes by a cost that weighs the prefill a worker would still have to do (the uncached part of the prompt) against that worker's decode load. SGLang's router keeps an approximate per-worker radix tree of past prompts. It sends a request to the best prefix match when the match rate passes a threshold, and to the shortest queue when load is imbalanced. Above that sit prefill/decode separation with KV transfer over RDMA (NIXL, Mooncake), autoscaling on SLO targets, and for the largest operators, wide expert parallelism across rack-scale NVLink domains. Aggregators such as OpenRouter route one level higher, across providers, by price, uptime and latency with fallbacks. That is why §4's headline price moves.

What our router already does. It discovers upstreams, resolves a model id to the widest topology serving it, and admits generation through one global first-in-first-out gate of width 12. That gate was tuned to the H200 knee in §2.

What a second island needs.

  1. A gate per upstream, not one global gate. A single width of 12 across both nodes would hold a B300 node at an eighth of its projected width. Each island needs its own width, measured on that node the way §2 measured this one.
  2. Session affinity when one model runs on both islands. 96.8% of our prompt tokens are served from the prefix cache. A turn routed to the wrong island prefills about 120,000 tokens instead of 3,700: about 12 s on H200 and about 2 s on B300. Our clients already send x-reckon-run-id and x-reckon-session, so pin each session to one island and fail over only when that island's gate is full or down. This is the same cache-affinity-with-load-fallback rule that the SGLang router and Dynamo implement.
  3. Prefer different models on different islands at first. Model-level routing needs nothing new, and keeps every cache warm.
