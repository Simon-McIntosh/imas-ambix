Rewritten in full on 2026-09-17. Every throughput, memory and usage figure in this version is measured on the currently running serve, after the prefix-cache repair landed. The previous version was argued against a lane whose token budget was nine-tenths avoidable recomputation; that premise is gone, and rather than amend the conclusions a third time the study is re-derived from a cost model fitted to the engine's own decode log.

§1 — The recommendation, and the one measurement it rests on

Yes, B-class is the right purchase, and the specification that matters most is not the letter. It is NVSwitch-connected SXM on an HGX baseboard. A bridged or PCIe Blackwell card would reproduce the exact interconnect problem we have now, one generation later and at three times the power. §5 is about that.

Everything else follows from one fit. Across eleven concurrency levels and more than eleven thousand decode intervals on the live serve, the time for one forward pass is:

milliseconds per pass = 17.8 + 0.257 × (gigabytes of expert weights that pass must read)

Two terms, and they behave completely differently:

  • A 17.8 ms floor, paid once per pass whatever the batch — 445 µs per layer across forty layers. This is collective latency, kernel launches, the sparse-attention indexer and expert dispatch. No amount of memory bandwidth reduces it.
  • 0.257 ms per gigabyte, which is 3.9 TB/s of effective bandwidth against 19.2 TB/s of nameplate — 20% of peak. This term is what a faster card attacks, and it is the larger one at every width we actually run.

At our operating point — 34 concurrent requests, 79.1 ms per pass — the split is 17.8 ms of floor and 61.3 ms of bandwidth. So bandwidth is 78% of the cost, which is the case for buying it, and the remaining 22% is a hard stop that grows in relative terms the more bandwidth you buy. Carrying the fit forward:

Changems per passAggregate at 34 concurrentVersus today

Read the last row as the warning it is. Doubling bandwidth a second time adds 20% because the fixed floor has taken over. A 3.33× card buys roughly 2.2× to 2.9×, not 3.33×, and past that the constraint stops being silicon we can buy.

Which is why two free software experiments come before any order, and both change the multiplier above. Speculative decoding attacks the floor — it emits several tokens per pass, so it divides the 17.8 ms rather than competing with it, and it is the one lever that is complementary to the purchase rather than an alternative to it. And the flashinfer_mxfp4_moe_precision sweep measures the size of the block-scale dequantisation tax directly, which is the difference between the 2.2× row and the 2.9× row. Both are one restart each. Ordering hardware before running them means guessing at a number we can measure.

§3 — Decode cost: a latency floor plus a bandwidth term

A mixture-of-experts model reads a different amount of weight per forward pass depending on how many requests are in the batch, because each token routes to its own experts and the batch must read the union of them. That is what makes the decode curve legible: concurrency is also a bytes-per-pass axis.

DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash is 40 layers, hidden size 5,120, MoE intermediate 2,304, 384 routed experts with top-6 plus one shared. One expert in one layer is 35.4 MB at FP8, so a single token's route costs 9.9 GB per pass across all forty layers. A batch of 34 tokens touches an expected 159 distinct experts per layer, and costs 227 GB.

Three things in that table decide the purchase.

One. The ratio to roofline converges. It is 54.6× at a single stream and settles at a flat 6.7–6.8× from sixteen concurrent upward. A constant ratio across a fourfold change in bytes is the signature of a fixed cost plus a proportional one, and it is what licenses the two-term fit rather than a hand-waved efficiency factor.

Two. Per-pass time falls from batch 1 to batch 4 — 28.2 ms to 24.4 ms — while the bytes read triple. Nothing about memory explains that. It is the floor being amortised, and it is why a single stream gets 35.5 tok/s on hardware that delivers 41.4 to each of four.

Three. We achieve 20% of nameplate HBM bandwidth. That is the number a Blackwell purchase can attack twice over: once by raising the nameplate 3.33×, and once by raising the fraction, if §6's block-scale account is right.

What the fit is and is not. It is two parameters over eleven points with a residual RMS of 3.8 ms against values spanning 24–79 ms. The expert count is the expected union under uniform routing; real routing is skewed toward popular experts, so the true bytes are somewhat lower and the true effective bandwidth somewhat worse than 20%. The floor's composition is not established: collective latency, kernel launch, the DSA indexer at index_topk 512 over 2,048 candidate blocks, and expert dispatch are all candidates and this data cannot separate them. The decomposition is load-bearing; the attribution of the floor is not, and no conclusion here depends on it.

§4 — HBM, and why it is the quantity that scales

HBM is High Bandwidth Memory. Ordinary graphics memory (GDDR) sits in packages beside the GPU and talks over a narrow bus clocked extremely fast. HBM instead stacks DRAM dies vertically, bonds the stack to the same package as the GPU through a silicon interposer, and connects it with a very wide bus — 1,024 bits per stack — clocked modestly. Wide-and-slow beats narrow-and-fast for total bytes per second and for energy per byte, which is why every serious accelerator uses it, and it is why capacity and bandwidth arrive together: both are properties of how many stacks of what generation are bonded to the package. You cannot buy one without the other, which matters for §7.

Why it is the decode quantity. Generating one token requires pushing the activated weights through the arithmetic units once. The arithmetic is trivial by comparison — a matrix-vector product per weight — so tokens per second is, to first order, bytes per second divided by bytes per token. That is the §3 slope. Prefill is the opposite: many tokens share one weight read, so prefill is compute-bound and its figures say nothing about decode. Most published throughput numbers are prefill-flattered; ours are not, which is why this study quotes its own.

§5 — NVLink: our bottleneck is the form factor, not the generation

NVLink is a point-to-point GPU-to-GPU interconnect. A single "link" carries 25 GB/s each way on the Hopper generation and 50 GB/s on Blackwell's fifth generation. A card exposes some number of links, and how they are wired is a property of the board, not of the chip. There are two wirings, and the difference is the most consequential thing in this document:

WiringWhat it givesWhere it appears
Bridges (2- or 4-way) A fully connected clique inside each bridged group and no NVLink path at all across groups — traffic between groups falls back to PCIe, an order of magnitude slower and host-mediated. H200 NVL — what we bought. Also PCIe Blackwell cards.
NVSwitch baseboard All eight GPUs in one all-to-all domain at full rate: 900 GB/s per card on Hopper SXM, 1.8 TB/s per card on Blackwell. HGX and DGX boards — H100/H200 SXM, B200, B300, GB200/GB300.

What we measured tonight, and what we still have not. Reading the topology inside the serve's own allocation, all four of its cards are NV6 to one another — a complete clique of six links per pair, 159 GB/s per direction. That confirms the four-card group is fully connected and confirms these are bridged NVL parts at a 600 W cap. It does not confirm the two-island account of our eight-card results, because device isolation shows a job only its own cards and the discriminating read needs five or more cards in one allocation. That allocation has never been available while the serve is up. The honest state is: the clique is measured, the boundary between cliques is inferred from two eight-card results that otherwise look like software faults — GLM-5.2's 16.4× aggregate collapse without drafting, and DeepSeek's +11.9% for four times the cards. Setting NCCL_DEBUG=INFO on the next eight-card launch would settle it for free, and no eight-card launch should happen without it.

Now the question that matters: does this problem transfer to B200? No — and that is a statement about boards, not about Blackwell. Our island boundary is a consequence of buying NVL bridged cards. An H200 SXM HGX node would not have it either. Conversely, a bridged or PCIe Blackwell card inherits it exactly, at 1,000–1,400 W per card instead of 600. The generation does not fix the wiring; the baseboard does. This is why §1 leads with the form factor rather than the letter, and it is the single most important line to get right in a request for quotation.

What NVSwitch is actually worth to us, stated narrowly. Inside one replica, tensor-parallel decode performs two all-reduces per layer — at batch 34 and hidden size 5,120 those are small transfers whose cost is latency, not bandwidth, so they sit in §3's 17.8 ms floor. NVSwitch shrinks that term and makes TP8 and EP8 viable at all, which is what GLM-5.3's 704 GiB FP8 release needs to fit. It does not buy much between replicas, because §6 of the tuning plan establishes that conversations should be pinned to a replica rather than migrated. So NVSwitch is bought for the floor and for the ability to run wide checkpoints, not for cross-replica traffic.

§6 — Four-bit and block scaling: the one advantage specific to our checkpoint

Blackwell is FP4-native, and that is not the same as INT4-native. The fifth-generation tensor cores implement FP4, FP6, FP8, INT8, FP16, BF16, TF32 and FP64. Native INT4 matrix instructions (IMMA.8832.S4.S4.SAT) existed on SM75–SM89 and were removed; on SM90 and SM100 INT4 operands reach the FP4 datapath through tcgen05.mma.kind::mxf4nvf4, i.e. by emulation. Anyone planning around "Blackwell INT4" is planning around a path that no longer exists as hardware.

MX means microscaling, an Open Compute Project standard for carrying a shared exponent per small block of values rather than per tensor. FP4 itself is E2M1: one sign bit, two exponent bits, one mantissa bit, sixteen representable values, an eighth the width of FP32. That is far too coarse on its own, so the format pairs it with a per-block scale:

FormatBlockScaleBits per element
MXFP432 elementsE8M0 (power of two)4.25
NVFP416 elementsE4M3, plus a per-tensor FP32 scale4.5

Here is the part that is specific to us, and it is the strongest Blackwell argument available. Our V4.1-Flash checkpoint declares its own quantisation as quant_method: fp8 with weight_block_size: [32, 32] and scale_fmt: ue8m0 — block-scaled FP8 carrying MX-format scales. On Hopper, a block-scaled recipe of this shape applies its scales in software, around the tensor core. On Blackwell the tensor core applies them itself, with no separate dequantisation kernel at all. Our serve runs the MoE through moe_runner_backend: flashinfer_mxfp4 at flashinfer_mxfp4_moe_precision: fp8, which is precisely that software path.

That is the most plausible large component of §3's measured 20%-of-peak effective bandwidth, and unlike a general FLOPS argument it applies to the exact weights we serve today. It is also testable before spending anything. The precision option takes three values — default (W4A16), bf16, and the fp8 (W4A8) we run — which move the work between the dequantisation path and the tensor core differently. If the block-scale tax is large, the sweep moves decode throughput measurably; if it does not move, the Blackwell-specific premium shrinks to the nameplate ratio and §1's 2.9× row goes away. One restart answers a question worth hundreds of thousands of euros.

The ecosystem argument sits on top of this and is real but softer: the checkpoints have already moved to where the hardware is, kernels are written Blackwell-first and back-ported to Hopper slowly or never, and we are currently on the wrong side of that gate — paying for it in workspace memory, in a twice-refused speculative decoder, and in a requantised GLM.

§7 — Capacity: what each card lets us stop working around

Capacity does not appear in the §3 cost model, so it buys no tokens per second. It buys the removal of specific, documented workarounds, and it buys concurrency headroom. Both are worth money; neither is throughput.

CardFree after one V4.1 rankWhat that removes
H200 NVL, 141 GB 33 GB loaded (52 idle) Nothing. Speculative decoding's ~12.9 GB fits with ~20 GB to spare; doubling the KV pool costs another 10.1 GB and leaves ~10. Two lazily-allocated requests in one restart is how three serves here have died.
B200, 180 GB ~72 GB Speculation and a doubled pool together, with margin for the workspace to grow under load rather than at the edge of failure.
B300, 288 GB ~270 GB The above, plus GLM-5.3's 704 GiB vendor FP8 release on four cards instead of eight — which retires the community INT4 requantisation and its damaged MTP head entirely. Plus a pool large enough that context ceases to be a design constraint.

The concurrency arithmetic, from measured numbers. KV costs 9.9 KB per token and a conversation holds 79,351 tokens, so one conversation is 0.80 GB of cache. Our 4M-token pool therefore holds about 50 concurrent conversations, and it reads 53% full at 27. A pool five times the size holds around 250. Note what that does not say: the §2 curve saturates at 8–16, so pool space past the knee buys the ability to hold conversations resident, not the ability to serve them faster. It removes silent prefix eviction, which is the first symptom of KV pressure and costs throughput before any counter moves.

Air carries roughly 30–40 kW per rack with a well-designed hot aisle, so an air-cooled eight-GPU Blackwell node at ~14 kW fits and two fit in one rack. The step from our node to either Blackwell option is a 2.9–3× increase in heat load in the same footprint — that is the number for the facilities conversation, not the GPU count. Three questions decide everything: can the rack take 14 kW, is there containment, and is there a coolant loop or a credible path to one.

Blackwell is the last NVIDIA generation with an air-cooled eight-GPU option. Vera Rubin NVL144 systems begin rolling out from the second half of 2026, but meaningful availability for institutional and smaller buyers is mid-2027 at the earliest — and liquid in every form factor, including the eight-GPU board. A decision to stay air-cooled is therefore a decision to buy Blackwell inside its window, or to buy nothing until the site changes.

§10 — What to do, in order, and what would change it

First, two software experiments, because both change the number you would be buying against.

  1. Speculative decoding. It attacks the 17.8 ms floor rather than the bandwidth term, which makes it complementary to the purchase rather than an alternative. Measured 45.3–46.4% acceptance at 3.31 mean accept length on the sibling checkpoint; expected 500–650 tok/s aggregate. Device memory now permits it. Owned by dsv41-serve-tuning-for-concurrency.
  2. The flashinfer_mxfp4_moe_precision sweep. Three values, one restart, and it measures §6's block-scale tax directly — the difference between §1's 2.2× and 2.9× rows.
  3. NCCL_DEBUG=INFO on the next eight-card launch. Free, and it settles §5's island boundary, which is one of the three pillars of the argument for leaving the NVL form factor.

Then the purchase, if the site can carry it.

  • 8×B200 on an NVSwitch HGX baseboard, air-cooled. The form factor clause is not boilerplate: a bridged or PCIe Blackwell card reproduces our current interconnect problem at 1–1.4 kW per card. Specify NVSwitch SXM explicitly.
  • B300 instead of B200 if— serving GLM-5.3's FP8 release on four cards is a goal, or the largest air-coolable memory pool is worth more than FP64. B300 is the better capacity buy and the worse general-purpose card.
  • Not another eight NVL cards. They would buy a second four-card machine, and §4 says the bandwidth that matters would be unchanged per replica.
  • The facilities answer is the gate. 14 kW in the rack, containment, and a coolant path. No GPU comparison substitutes for it, and the coolant loop is the long-lead item that makes the generation after this one possible at all.

What would change this recommendation.

  • The precision sweep shows no movement. Then the block-scale tax is small, the efficiency fraction will not improve on Blackwell, and the case narrows to the 2.2× nameplate row — at which point H200 SXM at €370k becomes genuinely competitive on price per delivered token, since it also removes the island boundary.
  • Speculative decoding delivers close to 3× alone. Then the urgency drops sharply: we would be at Blackwell-class aggregate on the cards we own, and the purchase becomes a capacity and capability decision rather than a throughput one.
  • The 17.8 ms floor turns out to be the sparse-attention indexer. Then neither card fixes it, the ceiling is roughly 3× whatever we buy, and the right response is a software investigation rather than a purchase order.
  • The lane's duty cycle stays diurnal. Two of every twenty-four hours measured had zero requests. If that persists, the justification is peak concurrency and capability, and it should be argued as such rather than on utilisation.
