§1 — The argument in one page

We have spent three weeks learning what four H200 cards cannot do, and every one of those lessons names a hardware property rather than a software mistake. That is what makes this a procurement question instead of an engineering backlog. Three properties account for almost all of it:

The recommendation is an air-cooled eight-card Blackwell node, and which Blackwell depends on one question we have not answered: whether this site can supply a coolant loop, and whether the new node must also carry fp64 physics. §12 states it properly. The rest of this document is the evidence, because the interesting part is not the conclusion — it is that our own measurements disagree with the obvious reason for buying. Generation speed is not our bottleneck. Our own record says it plainly: agentic wall throughput measures 22–33 tok/s while the engine peaks at 418.5, so "most of an agentic node's wall clock is not generation, and more generation capacity moves it less than the cache does." A purchase justified on tok/s would be justified on the wrong quantity. The one justified on capacity — context, prefix cache, concurrent workers, and models that fit at all — is the one the evidence supports.

Four readings in that table are worth more than the rest.

Eight cards are not twice four. DeepSeek moving from two cards to eight grows KV capacity 4.55× and aggregate throughput by 11.9%, while single-stream decode drops 18.3%. The GLM eight-card arm without drafting is worse than a joke: 45.7 tok/s single-stream, and 57.6 tok/s aggregate at 32 workers — it peaks at 60.6 with four workers and then stops scaling entirely. §5 argues that this is the NVLink island boundary rather than a software defect, and names the one cheap measurement that would confirm it.

The newest and most capable model is the slowest. V4.1-Flash decodes at 36.4 tok/s where V4-Flash manages 114.5 on the same four cards. We took that trade deliberately for a 5.3× larger prefix pool and a 512,000-token window, but it is the shape of things to come: each generation of open-weight frontier model is larger, sparser, and more dependent on hardware features we do not have.

Context is bought with memory, and we ran out. GLM-5.3's FP8 release is 703.7 GiB and needs all eight cards. To serve it on four we substituted a W4A8 requantisation of the same architecture — and its MLA latent KV is replicated on every rank rather than sharded, ~91.7 KiB per token across 78 layers, so the four-card deployment tops out at 98,304 tokens of context against a native window of 1,048,576. More cards buy context here only by shrinking weights-per-card, which is a memory-capacity argument and nothing else.

The synthetic numbers are not the service. V4-Flash delivers 86.6 tok/s per worker unloaded and 6.9–10.1 tok/s per worker on real agent traffic while carrying 12,000–17,000 tok/s of prefill. Figure 1 draws the gap. Any capacity projection in this study that ignores it is a vendor slide.

One live reading, taken while writing this. With real agent traffic flowing, the four cards report 96–100% utilisation at 392–406 W against a 600 W limit, at 61–63 °C. Utilisation that high means kernels are resident, not that the arithmetic units are saturated; a compute-bound FP8 GEMM on this part pulls far closer to the cap. The cards are waiting on memory and on a dequantisation path, and that is a hardware mismatch, not a thermal or power limit.

Read the memory row and the bandwidth row together, because they are the two that govern our workload. A Blackwell card holds 1.28× (B200) or 2.04× (B300) our weights-and-cache budget and reads it at 1.67× the rate. For a decode step, which streams a slice of the weights and the whole active KV set per token, bandwidth is very nearly the whole story — the arithmetic is trivially small. That ratio, 1.67×, is the most defensible single number in this study.

Now read the FP64 row, because it is the trap. The B300 buys its FP4 headroom by gutting double precision: NVIDIA publishes 10 TFLOPS of FP64 for HGX B300 against 34 on our H200. This repository's own GPU policy makes that binding rather than academic — imas_ambix/spine_bench/AGENTS.md defines a capability demonstration as running "on the accelerator, batched, in fp64, matching the host", and scripts/fsa_gpu_capability.py asserts float64 on its output. A B300 node would be a fine serving machine and a poor equilibrium-solver. B200 keeps FP64 roughly at parity with what we have.

§4 — 4-bit: Blackwell is FP4-native, and that is not the same as INT4-native

The premise this study was asked to confirm is half right, and the wrong half is the one that decides which checkpoints run fast. Blackwell's fifth-generation tensor cores natively execute FP4, FP6, FP8, INT8, FP16, BF16, TF32 and FP64 — and INT4 is absent from that list. Native INT4 matrix instructions existed from SM75 to SM89 (Turing through Ada) as IMMA.8832.S4.S4.SAT; compile the same operation for SM90 or SM100 and the compiler emits an emulation sequence built on INT8. What Blackwell added instead is a hardware FP4 datapath with block scaling — NVFP4 (E2M1 with a shared FP8 scale per 16 values) and MXFP4 (E2M1 with an E8M0 scale per 32 values) — reached through tcgen05.mma.kind::mxf4nvf4. An INT4 operand on SM100 rides that same FP4 machinery rather than a dedicated integer one.

So the correct claim is: Blackwell is the first architecture that multiplies 4-bit values in the format they are stored in. Everything before it either used an integer-only 4-bit path that the frontier checkpoints do not ship in, or converted 4-bit weights up to 8- or 16-bit before doing arithmetic.

This is not an abstraction; it is the direct cause of our most expensive week. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ships 259.5 GiB of MXFP4 routed experts. On our SM90 cards the native MXFP4 kernels are gated off — they exist only for Blackwell — so the engine falls back to a path that upcasts to BF16 and needs a fused-MoE workspace allocated lazily at generation: 9.00 GiB at a 300k prefill, 10.11 GiB at 500k. That workspace is what made a 20,000,000-token pool start cleanly, answer single requests, report 14.64 GiB free per card, and then die with SIGKILL under the first real load. It is also why speculative decoding is impossible here: DSpark asked for 12.00 GiB per card on top of everything else and was refused with 7.6 GB free even after we shrank the pool to its smallest load-proven size.

Our own record already contained the correct diagnosis and drew the wrong conclusion from it, which is worth preserving. We proposed a W4A16 requantisation to shrink the experts — and the experts were already four-bit, so it would have freed nothing. The surviving idea from that withdrawal was that a different kernel might beat the FlashInfer dequantisation path on SM90. The upstream work says the same thing and quantifies it: an opt-in path that losslessly re-encodes MXFP4 experts to block-FP8 at load time, to reach the FP8 tensor cores Hopper does have, measured 1.38–1.53× single-request prefill and 2.04× at 32 concurrent requests on TP4, at the cost of doubling expert weight memory, with accuracy inside noise. SGLang has a fused CUTLASS MXFP4-with-FP8-activations MoE backend for SM90 in the same spirit.

Two things follow, and they point in opposite directions. First, some of our gap is recoverable on the cards we own, for the price of one restart — which is an argument for measuring before buying (§12). Second, the fact that this workaround exists at all, and costs 2× weight memory on a card that has no memory to spare, is the argument for FP4-native silicon. On Blackwell the same checkpoint is simply multiplied, at twice the arithmetic rate of FP8, with no workspace and no penalty.

§5 — TP8, TP4, expert parallelism, and why our eight cards are two machines

Tensor parallelism splits every weight matrix across N cards. Each card holds 1/N of each layer, computes its slice, and then all N exchange partial results — an all-reduce per layer, twice per transformer block. It cuts weights-per-card by N and multiplies interconnect traffic by roughly the same factor. TP is therefore an interconnect trade, not a free scaling knob, and the quality of the link between the cards decides whether it works.

The width is not a free choice either. TP must divide the attention head count, which our own matrix study recorded the hard way: with 64 heads the feasible widths on this node are 2, 4 and 8, and six cards is not a valid arm. So the ladder is discrete, and the rungs are 2 / 4 / 8.

Expert parallelism (EP) is the MoE-specific alternative. Instead of slicing every expert across every card, EP gives each card whole experts and routes tokens to wherever their chosen experts live — an all-to-all per MoE layer rather than an all-reduce. Both our DeepSeek deployments run TP4 with EP4. For a model with 256 routed experts and 8 active per token this is much better behaved than pure TP, because each card does dense work on fewer experts. It also makes the interconnect latency-sensitive rather than merely bandwidth-hungry, since the all-to-all is small messages on the critical path of every token.

And here is the thing about our node that we appear never to have written down: its cards are H200 NVL, and an NVL bridge is at most four cards wide. A live topology read inside the serving job shows NV6 between each pair of its four cards — six of the card's eighteen NVLink lanes to each of three peers, 26.562 GB/s per lane, which is the 4-way bridge wired exactly as specified. NVIDIA's own specification for the part is a "2- or 4-way NVLink bridge". There is no 8-way option, and there is no NVSwitch on a PCIe card. Therefore an eight-card job on this node is two four-card NVLink islands connected only by PCIe Gen5, and a TP8 all-reduce must cross that gap twice per layer at roughly a seventh of NVLink bandwidth.

That is the best available explanation for the two eight-card results in §2 that otherwise look like software bugs: the 16.4× aggregate collapse of GLM without drafting, and DeepSeek's 11.9%-for-4×-the-cards. It is consistent with both, and with the 5,430-second load time of the eight-card GLM arm. It is an explanation, not a proof, and the measurement that would settle it is cheap: allocate all eight cards and read nvidia-smi topo -m. Two NVLink cliques with PIX or NODE between them confirms it; NV across all pairs refutes it. I could not run it while the serve holds four cards, and this study should not be read as having established it.

What this means for the purchase is unusually concrete. Any HGX or DGX baseboard — B200, B300, or an H200 SXM node — puts all eight GPUs in one NVSwitch domain at 900 GB/s (Hopper) or 1.8 TB/s (Blackwell) per card, all-to-all. TP8 and EP8 become genuine options instead of traps, which matters specifically for GLM-5.3, whose FP8 release needs eight cards to fit at all, and for any future checkpoint above ~600 GiB. Buying eight more NVL cards would buy a second four-card machine, not an eight-card one.

§6 — Memory is the scarce quantity, and it is scarce in a specific way

The engine's memory ledger on our final configuration, per card: 137.85 GB accounted available, 65.24 after weights, 55.15 after the 4,000,000-token pool, 52.62 at ready. Weights are ~75 GB of a 141 GB card. The pool costs ~10 GB per card for four million tokens — about 2.5 KB per token per card. What remains is not slack: it is the reserve the MoE workspace and the prefill chunk draw from, lazily, at the moment of largest allocation.

Free memory is not an abstraction to us; it is a list of things we could not have. At 42 GB free we cannot hold the Engram tables in HBM, cannot run speculative decoding at all, cannot raise the prefill chunk above 16,384 without shrinking something else, and cannot grow the device pool past 4M tokens without re-entering the region where a serve starts cleanly and dies under load. At 82 GB (B200) the DSpark workspace fits with room to spare. At 164 GB (B300) the device pool could hold roughly 20M tokens and the workspace and a doubled prefill chunk — or the whole GLM-5.3 FP8 release at TP8 with a large pool rather than a requantised substitute with 98,304 tokens of context.

And capacity, not speed, is what our workload actually consumes. The 8,000,000-token host prefix tier we added is worth more to a fleet of agent workers than any decode improvement, because agent turns re-read long prefixes. Our DSpark record states the general principle from measurement: the ceiling is turn count and thinking volume, so "more cards will not make an agentic node finish sooner" — but a bigger cache will, by removing prefill it would otherwise repeat. Every projection in §8 weights capacity accordingly.

If the answer on liquid is "no, and not soon", then the timing argument is settled for us. Blackwell is the last NVIDIA generation with an air-cooled eight-GPU option. Rubin is liquid in every form factor including the small one. A decision to stay air-cooled is therefore a decision to buy Blackwell within its availability window, or to buy nothing until the site changes.

§8 — Capacity scoping: concurrency, context, and honest throughput ranges

Multiplied naively that is 3.5–6.7× per-stream decode, so 130–240 tok/s single-stream and 2,800–5,300 tok/s aggregate at width 32, for V4.1-Flash on eight air-cooled Blackwell cards. §10 — The other options, including the ones we should not take

AMD MI355X — the best capacity per euro and the worst fit for our operations budget. 288 GB of HBM3E at 8 TB/s, native FP4, air-coolable on a standard UBB8 baseboard, and around 78 TFLOPS of FP64 — twice NVIDIA's, which our fp64 physics policy would welcome. The case against it is not the silicon, it is what we have just lived through. Getting GLM-5.3 to serve on CUDA took four attempts with four distinct causes, and one was an unmerged upstream architecture registration. The public record of DeepSeek-V4-Pro on MI355X is a 110.5× throughput improvement in 26 days across 31 optimisation pull requests — which is a triumph of engineering and simultaneously a measurement of how far the software was from ready at the start, on the flagship model, done by people who do that full-time. On identical FP4 workloads the same source puts B200 around 5× ahead in our serving range and attributes the gap to software rather than silicon. We would be adopting that gap along with the memory.

RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition — right architecture, wrong topology. 96 GB of GDDR7 at 1.6 TB/s, 600 W, passively cooled, FP4-native, and a fraction of the price. Eight of them total 768 GB, which on paper holds GLM-5.3's FP8 release. But there is no NVLink: every TP all-reduce and every EP all-to-all would cross PCIe, which is the exact pathology §5 blames for our eight-card collapse, at a seventh of the bandwidth and with no island to hide inside. For dense single-card inference, fine-tuning, or many small models it is excellent value. For a 744B-parameter MoE served across eight cards it is the failure we already have, bought deliberately.

Wait for Rubin — a real option, with a real cost. 288 GB of HBM4 at roughly 20 TB/s and ~50 PFLOPS of NVFP4 per package is a genuine generational step, and the platform is in production now with broad availability reported for 2026 Q4 into 2027 Q1. Two things argue against waiting. It is liquid-cooled in every form factor, so waiting only helps if the site is also getting a coolant loop; and it does not help the traffic we are serving today, on a serve whose context window we raised this week precisely because workers are hitting the limits we have.

Rack-scale NVL72 — out of scope, and worth saying why. GB300 NVL72 is 72 GPUs in one liquid-cooled NVLink domain at ~120–140 kW per rack. It is the right purchase for a fleet serving thousands of concurrent users. Our locked service statement is "a handful of concurrent consumers" on a shared node, and the honest reading of our own traffic is that we are nowhere near needing a rack.

Keep the H200 node either way. This is not a trade-in. Two four-card islands are an awkward eight-card machine and two perfectly good four-card serving lanes, and with 34 TFLOPS of FP64 apiece the cards remain the right home for the batched fp64 equilibrium work regardless of which accelerator arrives. If the new node is a B300, that argument becomes load-bearing rather than convenient.

§11 — Future-proofing, argued in both directions

For Blackwell now. The checkpoint formats have already moved to where the hardware is: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash ships MXFP4 experts and GLM-5.3 ships FP8, and the kernels that make either fast are written for Blackwell first and back-ported to Hopper slowly, partially, or never. We are currently on the wrong side of that gate and paying for it in workspace memory, in a refused speculative decoder, and in a requantised model. Buying FP4-native silicon puts us on the side the ecosystem optimises for, which is a durable advantage independent of any FLOPS number. NVLink 5 at 1.8 TB/s per card doubles the headroom for the parallelism these sparse models need. And it is the last generation that can arrive without a plumbing project.

Against Blackwell now. HBM3e at 8 TB/s is one generation from HBM4 at roughly 20 TB/s, and for a decode-bound workload bandwidth is the quantity that matters most; a 2026 Q4 purchase is a card whose defining number will be beaten by 2.5× within a year. The B300's FP64 collapse makes it a specialised part rather than a general one. Most Blackwell production is absorbed by hyperscalers, so institutional buyers face waitlists — a lead time that could push delivery into the window where Rubin is orderable. And our own evidence undercuts the urgency: if agentic wall clock is bound by turn count and thinking volume rather than generation, the fleet gets less from new silicon than the arithmetic suggests.

How to hold both. The resolution is not a compromise part, it is sequencing. Air-cooled Blackwell now is a five-year serving asset that works on the day it arrives and needs no facility change; Rubin, when it comes, is a liquid-cooled purchase that will require one anyway, and the coolant loop is the long-lead item. If the site is going to get liquid cooling eventually, the honest plan is: buy air-cooled Blackwell now for the workload we have, and treat the coolant loop as the infrastructure project that makes the next purchase possible.

§13 — Provenance

What this study does not establish. The two-island topology account of our eight-card results is an explanation consistent with three measurements, not a verified fact; §12 names the read that would settle it.
