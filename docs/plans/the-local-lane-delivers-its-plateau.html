§1 — What the lane delivers now

The router's request receipts for 16 to 18 November were replayed to reconstruct, second by second, how many requests the gate had in service and how many waited. Three facts come out of them, and they decide what this plan builds.

The lane's output is flat from about nine requests in service. One stream generates 118 tok/s alone and 39 tok/s when twelve run together, so all streams together deliver about 400 tok/s at six, 450 at nine and 470 at twelve. The gate's width of 12 already sits on the plateau, and a wider gate adds almost nothing: the plateau, not the width, is what the lane can deliver.

When work is queued, the lane already runs near that plateau. Between 04:45 and 06:40 UTC on 18 November, with a deep queue behind it, the gate averaged 11.10 requests in service of 12 and delivered 434 tok/s. Requests waited a median of 2.5 s at the gate and 11.0 s at the 90th percentile, and 62% of them waited more than a second. A panel reading of 7 in use and nothing waiting is one instant of a count that swings between about 7 and 12; the window is what shows the lane full.

The loss is in the hours the lane is underfed. In the two hours from midnight the same day the gate averaged 4.0 in service and delivered 232 tok/s; from 02:00 to 04:45 it averaged 1.7 and delivered 113 tok/s. Across 17 November the lane delivered 227 tok/s on average, about half its plateau, and ten of its 24 hours fell below 200 tok/s. No admission rule recovers those hours: nothing was asking for the lane.

Two defects in the published worker slots are real all the same. The slot figure counts every run that made a request in the last fifteen minutes as live, so finished runs hold slots until they age out, and a lane with fifteen live workers reads as holding twenty-four. And the figure is sized so that average demand equals the width, which leaves the gate below its width about a third of the time. Both are worth fixing, for a gain of a few percent in a fed hour and a faster refill when workers end. The larger gain needs the lane to say why it ran below its plateau, so the underfed hours can be traced to their cause.

The measurements, their commands and the scripts that produced them are in docs/evidence/fragments/the-local-lane-delivers-its-plateau/: occupancy-fed.log, occupancy-early.log, occupancy-starved.log, rate-by-load.log and hourly.log.

§2 — What a prototype of the admission rule shows

A simulation of the lane was calibrated from the same receipts: each worker's think time between requests, the tokens each request generates, the number of requests a run makes, and the per-stream rate at each load. Workers end and are replaced through a lane document published every 30 s and an admission pass every 30 s, with a launch taking 45 to 120 s to reach its first request. With a fixed 28 workers it delivers 430 tok/s at a median gate wait of 1.5 s, against the 434 tok/s and 2.5 s measured, so it reproduces the lane it was calibrated on.

A margin on the measured duty runs away. A worker's share of time in service falls as it waits longer at the gate, so a rule that divides the width by that share reads a fuller lane as room for more workers. With any margin above one it admits without bound: 83 workers at × 1.1 and 940 at × 1.25. This is the rule the earlier reading of the slot figures pointed towards, and the prototype rules it out.

Sizing from offered load is stable at every margin. The load a worker would offer with no gate wait is its service time over its service plus think time, with the time it spent waiting left out. That figure does not move as the gate fills, so the worker count settles at the margin times the width over that load. The margin is then a dial with a known cost: each step buys a few tok/s and a few seconds of gate wait.

It follows the workload. Think time averaged 20.7 s over the whole night and 6.5 s in the fed window, so the worker count that fills the lane differs by half. At a margin of 1.3 the rule settles on 30 workers for the first workload and 21 for the second, and delivers 442 and 441 tok/s. A fixed worker count tuned for one would starve or flood the other.

The simulated rule "as it stands" is not a measurement of the live lane: reckon's per-session shares and holds bound the live worker count in ways the simulation does not carry, so the live figures in §1 are the baseline. Gate widths of 10 and 16 were also simulated; 10 caps the lane at 400 tok/s, and 16 reaches 450 only on the assumption that output stays flat beyond twelve, which the receipts cannot test. The tables and logs are sim-w10.log, sim-w12.log, sim-w16.log, sim-offered-w12.log, late-rules.log and late-offered.log in the same evidence directory.

§3 — The lane simulation is a kept instrument that reproduces the measured lane

Deliverable. The repository holds a lane simulation and its calibration from request receipts as tested code, so a change to the slot rule is tried against the lane's measured behaviour before it reaches the router. The slot rule the simulation exercises and the ledger it reads are the router's own, called and not copied, so they cannot drift. The repository also holds, as test data, the receipt rows for three windows of 18 November (00:00 to 02:00, 02:00 to 04:45 and 04:45 to 06:40 UTC), taken from the router's receipt file with the caller address removed. The prototype scripts under the evidence directory stay as the record of §2 and are imported by nothing.

Done when. A test calibrates the simulation from the committed rows for 04:45 to 06:40 UTC and runs it with the worker count held at the mean live count of that window. The simulated delivered rate is within 5% of the 434 tok/s the excerpt records, and the simulated median gate wait is within 2 s of the 2.5 s it records. Negative control: the same run with every think time set to zero delivers the plateau at a median wait above 10 s, and the test fails on it. The result is logged to docs/evidence/fragments/the-local-lane-delivers-its-plateau/s3.html with the command and revision.

§4 — Worker slots follow the load live workers would offer

Deliverable. The lane document's worker_slots and live_runs describe the workers on the lane now. A run counts as live while it has a request waiting or in service, or made one within the working horizon the per-session share already uses; a run that ended earlier holds no slot. Capacity is the published margin times the gate's effective width, divided by the load one live run offers when it does not wait: its time in service over its live span less its time waiting at the gate. Capacity and the margin are published as their own fields; worker_slots stays what a dispatcher reads it as, the spare workers the lane can take now, which is capacity less the live runs. The margin is a setting in the gate's control file and defaults to 1.5: as this section's node measured in the §3 simulation and records in its evidence fragment, a margin of 1.25 leaves the starved workload at 95.9% to 98.9% of its plateau across three seeds, because a mean offer of fifteen requests leaves the lane under twelve in service part of the time, while 1.5 reaches the plateau on both workloads inside the wait bound. The live-run count, capacity, slots, the wait state of §5 and the utilisation figures of §6 are published as one block computed from one record of request intervals. The capacity sized from every run seen in the averaging window is removed, not kept beside this one.

Done when. Against a ledger of fifteen live runs and nine that ended more than the working horizon ago, the document reports fifteen live runs, and its capacity is within one of the margin times the width over the offered load computed by hand from the same ledger, and its published slots equal its published capacity less its published live runs. In the §3 simulation the rule's worker count, taken as ten-minute means after the first hour, stays within 15% of its mean over the last hour, across four hours at margins 1.0, 1.25 and 1.5 on both committed workloads, and at the default margin of 1.5 delivers at least 97% of the simulated plateau with a 90th-percentile gate wait under 15 s on both workloads. Negative control: the same simulation with duty measured over the live span including gate wait, at margin 1.25, exceeds 200 workers, and the test asserts that it does. Results are logged to docs/evidence/fragments/the-local-lane-delivers-its-plateau/s4.html.

§5 — A long gate wait closes the slots

Deliverable. The slot figure cannot keep admitting into a gate that is already making requests wait too long, whatever the offered load reads. When the 90th-percentile gate wait over the last five minutes exceeds a bound set in the gate's control file, 30 s by default, the document publishes zero slots and names the wait as the reason; slots reopen when the wait falls back under the bound. There is one place where the published slots go to zero and one field that says why: a paused gate and a long wait are both reported through it.

Done when. In the §3 simulation, a workload whose think time drops to a third at the two-hour mark stays under a 90th-percentile gate wait of 45 s in every five-minute window after the first ten minutes following the change, and the published document carries the wait reason in the windows where slots were closed. Negative control: the same run with the bound disabled exceeds 45 s in at least one window. A unit test covers the document fields at the bound, just under it and just over it, and shows a paused gate and a gate closed for wait differing only in the reason field. Results are logged to docs/evidence/fragments/the-local-lane-delivers-its-plateau/s5.html.

§6 — The lane document says how full the lane ran and why it fell short

Deliverable. The lane document carries, for its averaging window and for the last hour, the mean number of requests in service, the share of time the gate was at its width, the delivered completion-token rate, and the lane's plateau rate as estimated from its own receipts. It also splits the time the gate spent below its width into two parts the router can tell apart: time when it was offering slots that nobody took, and time when it was offering none. A reader can then tell an underfed lane from a throttled one without replaying receipts.

Done when. For the committed receipt excerpts of the fed window (04:45 to 06:40 UTC) and the starved window (02:00 to 04:45 UTC) of 18 November, the document fields computed by the router's code match the values the evidence scripts record for those windows: mean in service within 0.1, share at width within two points, and delivered rate within 2%. In the starved window at least 90% of the time below width is attributed to slots offered and not taken. In both windows the two attributed parts sum to the time below width within one second per hour, and the mean in service equals the figure the §4 block reports from the same rows. Negative control: a ledger in which the gate is paused attributes its time below width to no slots offered, and the test fails if it reads as offered. Results are logged to docs/evidence/fragments/the-local-lane-delivers-its-plateau/s6.html.

§9 — A session with queued work takes the slots idle sessions leave

Measured. At 11:25:32 UTC on 18 November the lane had 9 of 12 requests in service and none waiting. The document offered 7 worker slots lane-wide across five working sessions with a fair share of 6. One session held 285 queued requests and 6 runs inside the working horizon, and was offered 0 slots. The other four sessions held 1 or 2 runs each, had taken none of their offer, and were offered 4 or 5 slots each. The document holds back a reserve of 2 slots for every session below its share, so four such sessions reserved 8 slots against 7 free and the session with work was left none. Two defects met in that reading: the 7 free slots came from a lane-wide count of 25 runs that still included runs ended within the window, which §4 corrects, and the reserve was held once per session, which this section corrects. The lane fell from 11 in service to 7 over the following minutes while that queue stood. A copy of the document is kept at docs/evidence/fragments/the-local-lane-delivers-its-plateau/lane-borrow-reserve.json.

Deliverable. The slots the document holds back for sessions below their share total at most one borrow reserve across the lane, however many such sessions there are. A session at or above its share is therefore offered every free slot beyond that one reserve, and a session below its share is still offered room to grow toward it. Each session's run count and the lane-wide count come from the one record §4 defines, so the per-session block and the lane-wide block cannot disagree about which runs are live.

Done when. A unit test builds the document for a capacity of 16 with five sessions holding 6, 1, 2, 1 and 1 live runs, so 11 runs are live, 5 slots are free and the fair share is 3: the session holding 6 is offered at least 3, and each session below its share is offered at least its room up to that share (2, 1, 2 and 2). Negative control: the same input with one reserve held per session reserves 7 slots against 5 free and offers the session holding 6 nothing. The §3 simulation gains sessions as part of this section: every simulated worker belongs to a session, and a session launches only from the offer the document makes it. In that simulation, one session with an unbounded queue beside four sessions that each hold one worker and no queue delivers a completion-token rate within 5% of the same workload run as a single session, and the per-session reserve falls short of that single-session rate by more than 5%. Results are logged to docs/evidence/fragments/the-local-lane-delivers-its-plateau/s9.html.
