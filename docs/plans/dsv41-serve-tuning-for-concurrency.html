The prefix-cache defect is repaired and the lane is working. Everything below is measured against that repaired lane, not against the collapsed one, and the distinction matters because the pre-repair figures made the device pool look enormous and the host tier look useful. Both conclusions inverted when reuse went from under 5% to over 99%. Full record: the four-card throughput evidence.

§2 — The window question is closed: the serve is already down

As of 2026-09-17 the lane drained and the serve was shut down, so this section's original problem no longer exists. There is nothing to rotate around, no dispatch to halt and no lull to find — the restart is a cold launch. Everything below is kept because it is the record of what a live rotation would have cost and because the duty-cycle table is the instrument for the next restart, not this one.

What binds now is cores, not cards. Four cards are free. The group-A reservation holds thirty cores, of which the router keeps 2 and two released one-card nova jobs keep 8 each, leaving roughly 12–14. The profile asks for 12. So the launch fits with almost no margin, and a job pending on Resources after submission means cores rather than cards — read the live ledger before blaming the GPU count.

The rotation was also the safety net, and losing it changes the risk. With the old engine still serving, a speculative decoder that refused to start cost nothing. Now a failed launch means no lane at all until a rollback completes. So the rollback needs a time bound rather than a judgement call: if the serve has not answered /v1/models within about twelve minutes — against a measured 460 s to ready — relaunch without the speculative settings to bring the lane back, and treat DSpark as a separate attempt once traffic is flowing again.

The remainder of this section is the superseded rotation analysis, retained for the next restart.

A zero-downtime rotation is supported and was demonstrated on 2026-09-07. Bring the retuned serve up on its own port while the current one keeps serving, let the router move traffic on health, then stop the old one. Two properties make it work, and both are in the code rather than in an operator's care: the router collapses a duplicate model id onto one upstream instead of refusing, and the endpoint document republishes on both serve and shutdown.

The equal-width tie-break is already correct, which the demonstration did not prove. That rotation replaced two cards with four, so the documented rule — prefer the widest — decided it. A retune is four cards replacing four cards, and the width term is level. _preferred_owner in imas_ambix/agent/router.py resolves exactly this: level widths fall through to the later start stamp, which still favours the engine replacing its peer, and it refuses to choose only when both terms are indistinguishable. So the new serve wins the tie by construction. This needed checking rather than assuming, because a rotation that split traffic across two engines would give each of them half the conversations and halve the prefix reuse — the §6 failure mode, arriving unannounced during what was supposed to be a safe procedure.

What the rotation actually costs is four free cards for about eight minutes. Measured on the last restart: 460 s from submit to the first successful /v1/models. The node is the constraint, not the lane — at the time of writing the serve holds 4 cards, a nova-bank-drift-campaign job holds 2, and 2 are free. So the rotation is available when the node is otherwise empty and not before.

And if the cards are not free, the quiet window already exists on a schedule — it does not have to be negotiated. 3,004 twenty-second samples over 16.8 h give the lane's own duty cycle, and it is strongly diurnal:

A drain, if one is ever needed, is about a minute. Mean end-to-end request latency over the night is 54.6 s across 7,232 requests, and 51.4 s in the most recent window; mean output is 855 tokens. So holding dispatch and waiting for in-flight requests to retire costs one to three minutes, not the tens of minutes a run-level drain would — a worker between turns is not holding a request. Never conflate the two: requests drain in a minute, runs drain when their node ends.

Done when: the retune lands either through a rotation on a free four-card window or through a coordinated eight-minute hold, with the method used and the in-flight request count at cutover recorded. A restart that kills live requests without a recorded hold is a defect in this section, not an acceptable cost.

§3 — Lever 1: DSpark speculative decoding, the only real throughput lever

Decode binds this lane and nothing else in the configuration changes that. Per-worker output is 27.0 tok/s median against a single-stream ceiling of 34.4; the pool is half empty, the queue is empty, and reuse is at 99.5%. There is no prefill lever left to pull and no admission lever worth pulling. What remains is emitting more than one token per forward pass.

It was refused twice, for a reason that has since gone away. DSpark wants about 12 GiB per card of workspace, allocated lazily outside mem_fraction_static. At the 15M-token pool there was 1.3 GiB free; at the 7.6 GB that followed, still not enough. The refusal was a memory fact, and the memory fact has changed — but the margin must be read loaded, not idle:

The ~19 GB difference is the lazily-allocated workspace this section is about, so the headroom DSpark has to fit inside is 33 GB and not 52. It still fits: 33 − 12.9 ≈ 20 GB. But the earlier figure was an idle reading quoted as a budget, which is the same error as reading a fleet's context right after a dispatch, and the tighter number is the one that governs.

What changed is not only the memory — it is the value. Before the prefix repair this lane spent essentially its whole token budget on prefill, so accelerating decode would have bought almost nothing. Measured over the last 40 minutes, uncached prompt tokens are 2.3% of those presented and the engine generates 336 tok/s against 1,178 tok/s of uncached prefill. Decode is now the phase that costs wall clock, because each of its tokens needs its own forward pass. The lever became feasible and worth pulling in the same repair.

The profile's own comment says this will not help, and that objection has to be answered rather than ignored. It records prior experience on V4-Flash, where DSpark did not change throughput materially, alongside the capacity refusal. The capacity half is stale — it reasons from a pool costing ~16 GiB of base plus 1.6 KiB/token, where the running pool measures 10.10 GB for four million tokens — but the benefit half is a recorded null on the sibling checkpoint and this plan previously talked past it.

The decode cost model reconciles the null with the acceptance measurement, and predicts something falsifiable. Speculation verifies k draft tokens in one forward pass, so it pays the 17.8 ms floor once instead of k times — but the pass must read the expert union over k×B tokens instead of B, and that union grows fast. Carrying the measured 3.31 mean accept length at k=5 through ms = 17.8 + 0.257 × GB, with a 10 ms allowance for the five sequential draft steps:

The shape is the finding: the gain is large at a single stream, collapses to nothing around the knee, and returns at high width. At one stream the floor is the entire cost, so paying it once instead of 3.31 times is nearly free throughput. At width 8 the expert union goes from 12% to 47% of the 384 experts — 3.9× the bytes for 5× the tokens — and the bandwidth term eats the floor saving. At width 34 the union is already 41% saturated, so verifying five times the tokens costs only 2.24× the bytes and the saving survives.

So a null measured near the knee is exactly what this model predicts, which is the most likely explanation of the V4-Flash result and the reason a single-width test is worthless here. Measure at 1, 8 and 24 or above. A test at width 8 alone would reproduce the recorded null and wrongly close the question for a second time.

The dominant unknown is the drafter's own cost, and it can make this a regression. The 10 ms allowance above is an assumption, not a measurement: five sequential draft steps over three layers (dspark_target_layer_ids [37, 38, 39], 128 experts at top-3). At 25 ms the width-8 case becomes 0.92× — slower than today. Record the draft overhead per step, not only the acceptance rate, or the result cannot be interpreted at the widths where it matters.

The caveat is specific and must not be softened: on V4.1 it previously could not start. That was at a pool leaving no room for the workspace, so the memory explanation covers it, but no one has seen DSpark run on this checkpoint. Two failure shapes are possible and only one is safe: a serve that refuses at startup costs nothing, while a serve that starts and dies on the first real generation takes the lane with it — the lazy-allocation shape that has killed three serves on this node. So the acceptance test is a real generation, not a readiness probe.

This is a profile edit and not a code change, which is what makes it dispatchable in one session. speculative_algorithm and speculative_dspark_block_size already exist as SGLang-only fields in imas_ambix/agent/profile.py and are already emitted as --speculative-algorithm and --speculative-dspark-block-size by imas_ambix/agent/slurm.py. The values are "dspark" and 5, the second taken from the checkpoint's own dspark_block_size. The draft module is fused into the checkpoint at layers 37–39, so no separate draft path is needed and the commented vLLM-shaped settings in the V4-Flash profile are the wrong names for this engine.

Done when: DSpark is enabled in imas_ambix/agent/profiles/deepseek-v4-1-flash.toml, the serve reaches ready, and a real multi-hundred-token generation completes; with the measured draft acceptance rate, mean accept length, the device free memory after the workspace has actually allocated, and per-worker plus aggregate output under real load at a stated width. A rollback to the committed profile is one edit and is written down before the restart, not after it fails.

§4 — Levers 2 and 3: give back host RAM, then buy device pool with what §3 leaves

These are one section because they are one argument about where memory should sit, and two restarts because their risks do not belong together.

Lever 2 — hicache_ratio 8.0 → 2.0. A cost lever with no expected throughput change. The host tier writes and does not read: 100,308 backups against 56 load-backs. Every evicted page is copied to host RAM at full rate and then recomputed through the model anyway. At ratio 2 the tier is 86.0 GB across five pools per rank, so ratio 8 is holding roughly 344 GB for a store nothing reads. Dropping to 2 frees about 258 GB and takes the job's memory request from 720G to 480G. Expected throughput change: zero. Expected benefit: the node stops reserving a third of its RAM for nothing, which is what makes §6 arithmetically possible at all.

Lever 3 — max_total_tokens 4M → 8M. A concurrency lever, now load-bearing rather than speculative. §1's reading is the whole argument: 52–54% occupancy at 27 concurrent, ~79k tokens resident per request, so the pool binds near 50. Doubling it costs a measured rather than estimated amount — the startup log puts the 4M pool at 10.10 GB per card, so 9.9 KB per token across the four ranks, and another 4M costs another 10.10 GB per card — and moves that bound to ~100, which is past anything the dependency graph has ever offered. Expected throughput change at today's width: none. What it buys is the absence of silent eviction at the widths §3 and §6 make reachable.

Why lever 3 waits for a second restart, and this is the one sequencing decision in the plan. DSpark's 12 GiB per card and the pool's 10 GB per card are both device memory, and the budget takes both: 52 − 12 − 10 ≈ 30 GB spare. But the DSpark workspace allocates lazily, so its real footprint is unknown until a generation runs, and putting two untested device allocations in one restart is precisely the combination that has killed serves here. Lever 2 is host-side and confounds nothing, so it rides with §3. Lever 3 is sized from the free memory §3 actually measures.

Done when: restart A carries §3 plus hicache_ratio = 2.0 and memory = "480G", with the host-tier allocation lines counted against pools × ranks to confirm the tier is the size claimed; restart B raises max_total_tokens to a value justified by restart A's measured free device memory, with pool occupancy re-read at a stated width afterwards.

§6 — Eight cards are two TP4 replicas, and the routing has to land first

Deferred. Trigger: §3 has landed and the lane is measurably saturating at its new knee. Recorded now because the eight-card question will be asked again and the answer is already evidenced.

TP8 is refused on measured grounds, not on preference. These are H200 NVL cards: the bridge joins four, and the two bridged groups reach each other over PCIe Gen5 — NV6 within a group, measured on this node. A TP8 group's all-reduce therefore leaves NVLink and comes back, twice per layer. The precedent is direct: V4-Flash from two cards to eight gave +11.9% aggregate for −18.3% single-stream, and GLM-5.2 at TP8 without MTP collapsed to 57.6 tok/s. Twice the silicon for a slower token.

Two TP4 replicas keep every all-reduce inside an island and double the device pool. Expected aggregate 660–750 tok/s with a knee around 40–54 concurrent. Host RAM fits once lever 2 has landed: two Engram allocations at 183 GiB plus two host tiers at 86 GB is about 636 GB of the node's ~1,300 GB available — which is unaffordable at hicache_ratio 8.0 and comfortable at 2.0.

The hard prerequisite is session-affinity routing, and without it this option recreates tonight's defect at twice the cost. Each replica caches only the conversations it served. A worker whose turns alternate between replicas gets a cold prefix every time — the same 0%-reuse condition the router repair just fixed, arriving from a different direction and costing eight cards instead of four.

It is a configuration, not a feature to build. The serving container already carries sglang_router 0.3.2, whose default policy is cache_aware: it maintains an approximate radix tree of what each worker holds and sends a request to the worker with the longest matching prefix, falling back to load balancing when the match is under --cache-threshold (0.3) or when workers diverge past --balance-abs-threshold (64 requests) and --balance-rel-threshold (1.5×) together. Cheaper policies for the same goal sit beside it — consistent_hashing and prefix_hash pin by hash without tracking cache state — and the prefill/decode split has its own --prefill-policy and --decode-policy. So the gate below is satisfied by standing up a tested upstream router in front of two engines, not by writing affinity into imas_ambix/agent/router.py.

And affinity is not the only way; it is the cheap way. A conversation's KV at this lane's working context is 79,351 tokens × 9.9 KB = 0.80 GB, so moving one between replicas is tens of milliseconds on any interconnect in this machine, against ~28 s to recompute it at the 2,820 tok/s uncached-prefill rate the night actually sustained. Transfer beats recompute by roughly three orders of magnitude, which is why shared-KV tiers exist at all — hicache_storage_backend and the Mooncake-backed unified cache linker are both present and unset in the running configuration. Pin first, because pinning costs nothing and captures nearly all of it; reach for transfer only when a replica dies, load skews, or a replica is added mid-flight.

And the honest comparison is the reason this is deferred rather than queued: §3 is predicted to reach ~710 tok/s at width 34 on the four cards we own for the price of one restart, while §6 buys 660–750 for four more cards plus a routing feature. Per card, DSpark wins outright — and unlike §6 it attacks the latency floor, which is the term no extra hardware reduces.

§ Decisions

Do all three configuration levers ride one restart, or does the device pool wait for a second?

Locked from the record rather than from preference, because the two candidate changes compete for the same memory and one of them allocates lazily. DSpark asks about 12 GiB per card and the pool doubling about 10 GB per card, both device-side, against 52.04 GB free — so they fit on paper with roughly 30 GB spare. But the DSpark workspace is allocated at generation time outside mem_fraction_static, so its real footprint is unknown until a token is generated, and a lazily-allocated workspace meeting a just-doubled pool is the exact combination that has killed three serves on this node. A combined restart that dies on first generation also cannot say which change caused it. Lever 2 is host-side, frees 258 GB, confounds nothing and is a precondition for the eight-card option, so it rides with DSpark at no risk. Lever 3 is then sized from a measured number instead of an estimate. The cost of this choice is one extra restart window, which section 2 establishes may not require downtime at all.

§ Followups

Land restart A — DSpark plus the host-tier cut — through a rotation if four cards are free, and prove it with a real generation

Check the card ledger before scheduling anything. §2's finding is that the restart probably needs no quiet window: a four-card rotation on a spare port is supported, the equal-width tie-break in _preferred_owner resolves toward the incoming engine, and the cutover costs 460 s of overlap rather than any downtime. It needs four free cards. At the time of writing the node holds the 4-card serve plus a 2-card nova job, leaving 2 — so read squeue -w 98dci4-gpu-0003 first, and fall back to a coordinated eight-minute dispatch hold with the efit and nova sessions only if the cards are not there.

Restart A is DSpark plus hicache_ratio = 2.0 plus memory = "480G", and nothing else. The pool stays at 4M by decision — see the locked restart-grouping decision; do not fold §4's lever 3 in.

Readiness is not the measure. DSpark's workspace allocates lazily at generation, so the acceptance test is a real multi-hundred-token completion, and the numbers to record are draft acceptance rate, mean accept length, device free memory after the workspace has allocated, and per-worker plus aggregate output at a stated concurrency width. The rollback to the committed profile is written down before the restart, not improvised after it. If DSpark refuses to start on this checkpoint a second time, that is a real outcome: record the memory figures beside the refusal and leave lever 2 in place.

Sweep the MoE precision option, because it measures a tax worth hundreds of thousands of euros

This is a fifth lever, and it is the cheapest decision-relevant measurement available. The decode cost on these four cards fits ms per pass = 17.8 + 0.257 × GB of expert weights read over eleven concurrency levels, and that slope is 3.9 TB/s of effective bandwidth against 19.2 TB/s of nameplate — 20% of peak. The leading candidate for the missing 80% is the block-scale dequantisation our checkpoint forces: it declares weight_block_size [32,32] with scale_fmt ue8m0, and Hopper applies such scales in software around the tensor core where Blackwell applies them inside it.

The sweep tests that without buying anything. flashinfer_mxfp4_moe_precision takes default (W4A16), bf16 and the fp8 (W4A8) now in force, which distribute work between the dequantisation path and the tensor core differently. Record aggregate and per-request output at a fixed concurrency width for each value, plus the per-pass time at two widths so the fit can be re-run per arm. A large movement localises the tax and sets the expected Blackwell gain at roughly 2.9×; no movement narrows it to the nameplate ratio of 2.2× and makes H200 SXM competitive on price per delivered token.

Sequence it after the speculative-decoding arm and hold everything else fixed, including the pool, so the arms differ in one setting. Also free and worth taking whenever eight cards are next allocated for any reason: set NCCL_DEBUG=INFO on that launch. It settles whether the eight cards are two NVLink islands, which is a pillar of the accelerator study's form-factor argument and has never been measurable while the serve holds four.
