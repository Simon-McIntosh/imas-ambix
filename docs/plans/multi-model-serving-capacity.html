§1 — The keyless relaunch (landed)

An open endpoint is now what a serve produces without argument, and the running DeepSeek serve is the first one launched that way. The reasoning is that the cluster is already the authentication boundary — only users who can route to the compute node reach the port — so a key buys capacity accounting rather than confidentiality, and it costs something concrete: the shared key is readable only inside one storage group, so a keyed endpoint locks the standalone launcher out of the very endpoint it exists to reach. The world-readable launcher mirror was unusable for anyone outside that group for exactly this reason.

What landed:

  • serve --no-auth replaced by serve --auth; keyless is the default. Naming a key arms enforcement on its own rather than being silently discarded, and asking for auth with no resolvable key is a launch error rather than a quiet downgrade to an open port. The retired negative spelling fails loudly.
  • restart had kept resolving a key unconditionally, which would have restored a keyed endpoint the next time anyone used it. Both commands now share one auth resolution.
  • A --cpus override, because cores rather than cards are the scarce resource here and the count that scales proportionally with cards reserves cores a co-running job could use.
  • The relaunch itself: 2 cards and 8 cores, probed against the scheduler before committing, leaving 7 of the 30 reserved cores free rather than the 15 the previous launch held.

The launcher's 401 failure had one cause and this removes it. Confirming clive --list against the relaunched endpoint is a done-when item for serving-endpoint-indirection §5, not a claim made here.

§2 — The second engine, and where it is owned

The concurrent configuration is the four-card INT4 variant beside the existing two-card DeepSeek serve — 6 of 8 cards, two spare — because the vendor FP8 release fills the node and so is an alternative to concurrency rather than part of it. Both checkpoints are on disk.

The bringup and its wiring are owned by glm-53-serve-and-wiring, which is a prerequisite of the router rather than a consequence of this plan: nothing here can be measured until a second engine is listening. What stays here is what the second engine makes newly answerable — the budget that binds in §3, the admission limits in §4, and the service statement those imply.

One documentation correction belongs with the budget rather than the bringup: the sub-tree guidance described both GLM profiles as unusable stubs whose weights were unpublished. That was true when written and is now wrong in a way that would stop someone serving a model sitting on disk, so it has been corrected alongside the core-budget rules it sits next to.

§3 — The budget that actually binds

Cards are not the constraint; cores are. The node carries two overlapping 30-core reservations, and an inference server spends most of its life blocked on the device, so a proportionally-scaled core count reserves capacity that a co-running job could use. A serve sized at the proportional default has previously left a training job pending on cores while every GPU it wanted sat idle.

Every serve is sized by probing the scheduler with a no-op allocation first, never by arithmetic on the profile default. The engine's own memory budget is separately measured at startup rather than predicted: this GLM architecture replicates a compressed attention latent on every rank rather than sharding it, so additional cards buy context only by shrinking weights per card, and the startup pool line is the ground truth for any context ceiling quoted afterwards.

§4 — Admission control, and what can be promised

The engine will not self-limit usefully. The DeepSeek serve runs with a 1024-sequence ceiling against a very compact KV cache, so it will accept far more concurrency than the node serves at acceptable latency. Admission control therefore belongs in the router, and this is the second reason that component earns its place.

In order of leverage:

  • Per-consumer concurrency cap with a bounded queue. A heavy consumer degrades their own latency instead of everyone's, and past the bound the router returns a retry directive rather than queueing without limit.
  • Capacity split by alias. An interactive serve and a batch serve, selected by which alias a request arrives under. This is the second use of the alias mapping built in clive-launch-modes.
  • Per-consumer accounting in the router's own log, which is the same mechanism that keeps consumer metadata out of the operator's job log.

None of this manufactures capacity. Eight cards is eight cards, and if demand exceeds them the honest answer is a queue with a published wait rather than more knobs. That makes the audience question a real input rather than a detail: a group-sized service can run best-effort on spare cards, whereas anything wider needs a reserved floor that is not also the training pool, and the two imply different card budgets. It is recorded as an open decision because it sizes everything above it.

§5 — Done-when

  • Two releases serve concurrently on distinct ports inside the card and core budget, each sized from a scheduler probe rather than a scaled default.
  • Each engine's context ceiling is quoted from its own startup pool line, and each is validated by a real generation rather than by reaching a ready state.
  • A per-consumer concurrency cap is enforced with a bounded queue and a retry directive past the bound, demonstrated under deliberate overload.
  • The sub-tree guidance describes the GLM profiles as they now are.
  • A written service statement says who the audience is, what the reserved floor is, and what a consumer should expect when the node is busy.

§ Decisions

Does a serve enforce an API key unless told not to, or serve openly unless told to enforce one?

Locked to open by default. The cluster is the authentication boundary, so a key buys capacity accounting rather than confidentiality, while a key readable only inside one storage group locks the shared launcher out of its own endpoint. Where accounting matters it belongs on the router, which can hold a key without the consumer needing one.

Which GLM checkpoint is the concurrent deployment — the 4-card INT4 requant, or the 8-card vendor FP8 as a whole-node alternative?

Locked to the four-card INT4 requant as the concurrent deployment. The vendor FP8 release fills the node and so forecloses the multi-model surface this sprint exists for. Its single-model quality measurement is deliberately deferred rather than scheduled: taking that number means stopping the running keyless DeepSeek serve and a running physics job, and no multi-model result depends on it. FP8 is recorded as a whole-node alternative to concurrency, never a default deployment.

Is the served endpoint a group-sized best-effort service, or a cluster-wide service with a reserved floor?

Locked to a group-sized best-effort service. It serves the storage group on spare cards with no reserved floor, so a serve may be cancelled for a training campaign, and admission limits are sized for a handful of concurrent consumers rather than a published cluster-wide wait. This matches how the node is already shared. The service statement is written so widening later is a card-budget change rather than a redesign.
