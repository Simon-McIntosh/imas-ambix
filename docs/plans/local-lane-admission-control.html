§1 — What the lane is doing now

The DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash lane on 98dci4-gpu-0003 (four H200s, 512k context) was observed at 08:08 local on 2026-09-23 running 29 concurrent requests with zero queued, 217 tok/s aggregate output and 19.6 tok/s per stream, with a 133k median prompt, TTFT 1.7 s, KV pool at 78% and prefix-cache hit near 100%. The lane document published four minutes later read 31 running, 0 waiting, 79% KV occupancy and a host-tier cache at 97.5%. The engine was not refusing or queueing anything. It simply let every arrival into the running batch.

That is how the lane is configured. The engine profile (imas_ambix/agent/profiles/deepseek-v4-1-flash.toml) sets max_running_requests = 36, chosen to sit below the 4M-token pool's fill point at roughly 38–40 concurrent. The router's per-consumer admission filter was removed on 2026-09-14 (76a6a235) for good reason. It keyed consumers on host plus user-agent, so every harness on one login node shared a single six-slot bucket, and it refused 37.4% of an afternoon's requests while the engine sat idle. Since then the only bound anywhere in the path is the engine's 36. Reckon crew dispatch reads a published lane ceiling as advice, and nobody outside crew dispatch reads it at all.

The profile justifies 36 with a claim that aggregate decode is flat from width 17 to 32. A later measurement of the same lane contradicts it: aggregate output peaks near 22 concurrent at about 500 tok/s and falls to about 340 past it, while waiting stays at zero and the hit rate stays high. That is the failure the lead is describing. Past the knee, each additional stream buys no tokens and slows every other one. Nothing in the path queues work instead. Nothing tells a consumer outside crew dispatch that the lane is full, so a busy lane looks the same as a slow model.

§2 — The levers, by layer

This plan holds to one principle. Admit requests, not agents, up to the width that maximises aggregate tok/s, and let every other request wait in a queue that is cheap, fair and visible. An agent is mostly between requests, executing tools. A running cap of 22 therefore serves many more than 22 agents, provided queued requests hold no KV and are admitted in a sensible order.

§6 — The fix

This section is written after §3–§5 land, from their evidence. The leading candidate is to move the binding point to the engine: set max_running_requests at the measured peak width, select a prefix-aware schedule policy, and keep the router pass-through. The router gains a global bound only if the engine's queue proves unfair, blind to KV, or invisible to consumers. Any relaunch drains the lane to a genuine, sustained zero first. The running serve is never hard-stopped.

§7 — Immediate relief: one global in-flight gate at the router

A second reading at 08:30 local showed 33 running and 1 queued, with aggregate output down to 98 tok/s (from 217), 14.5 tok/s per stream, prefill at 1.95k tok/s, TTFT 3.0 s and the KV pool at 88%. The lane is past its throughput knee and close to prefix eviction, and about thirty agent sessions across several projects share it. None of them read a crew ceiling. Relaunching the engine with a lower max_running_requests needs a drain first, so the fastest queue on the serve side is the router, which is a separate job and restarts in seconds.

The gate is deliberately unlike the filter removed on 2026-09-14. It is one global bound, with no consumer identity and no per-consumer slots. Once the model catalog has resolved a request's owner, requests are admitted in first-in, first-out order, up to a width W. Order is by completion of that lookup, not by arrival at the router. A request that finds the gate full waits, and holds no engine KV while it waits. It is refused only after a wait bound set well beyond an ordinary turn, and the refusal is an Anthropic overloaded_error (HTTP 529) carrying Retry-After, so a client backs off rather than hanging. A request's slot is released in a finally when its stream ends or the client disconnects. Catalog and count_tokens requests bypass the gate, because they cost the engine no decode width. The router serves no health route. W and the wait bound are read at run time from a small operator-editable file, so retuning W after §3 lands needs no restart; if the file is absent the gate uses its launch defaults, and a width of 0 disables it. The router publishes the gate's in-flight and waiting counts in the lane document beside the engine's own.

Done when: a test drives more concurrent streams than W against a stub upstream and observes at most W upstream requests in flight while none is refused. A second test observes the 529 with Retry-After once the wait bound elapses. A third observes a disconnected waiter and a disconnected streamer both releasing their slots. A fourth observes that editing the file changes W on a running app. Deployment is a router restart, which drops the streams in flight. It is authorised separately and is not part of the node.

The width is not a fixed knee. Five days of decode samples binned by width (79,435 intervals, read by the slurm-hosted-agent-fleet session) show aggregate decode flat at 346–408 tok/s from width 10 to 35 at that traffic's contexts. The live collapse happens at the same widths only because today's contexts are about 99k tokens. The binding variable is KV occupancy, width times context: 4M pool tokens over a 98,750-token mean context is 40 requests at full occupancy, and at 35 running the pool is 85% pinned by running requests. That leaves too little room for idle agents' prefixes, which spill into a host tier that is 99.86% full and come back as recomputed prefill. So W tracks the lane document's own context-derived concurrent_requests, not a constant. Flat decode across widths 10–35 means lowering W costs almost no decode throughput.

  1. Disconnect release is proved, not assumed. In 1 of 5 runs, the pass-through test test_sse_bytes_are_identical_and_disconnect_closes_upstream timed out on the integrated head. The wait bound has to beat the client's first-byte window, not only its request timeout. Claude Code 2.1.280, the build clive and crew workers both run, retries 408, 409, 429 and every 5xx ten times by default. It follows Retry-After exactly, uncapped, in place of its own back-off. Its request timeout is 600 s (API_TIMEOUT_MS). While the gate holds a streaming request, the router sends no headers, so the stream's first-byte window (CLAUDE_STREAM_FIRST_BYTE_TIMEOUT_MS) may be the bound that actually applies. Its default is not yet established. If it is shorter than the gate's wait, a queued request dies client-side as a timeout and is not retried. Two designs follow from that answer. If the window is long, the wait bound goes below it. If it is short, the gate opens the stream as soon as it accepts a request. It then emits the Anthropic ping event while the request waits, and ends with an SSE overloaded_error event if the wait expires. That trades the 529 status for a stream that stays alive in the queue.

The source of W needs a floor and a slow estimator. The lane's concurrent_requests swung 16, 17, 20, 6, 20, 18 and 19 across one night. The 6 came at 01:00 with the fleet drained, when a few long-context runs moved a short-window median. A gate that followed that figure directly would throttle the fleet for reasons no operator would recognise. The process that writes the gate file therefore derives W from pool tokens over a slow-moving context estimate, clamps it between a floor and the engine's own cap, and never reads sizing_verdict or headroom_is_upper_bound. Both are measured to vary between reads.

No client has ever been able to read the lane's ceiling. Twelve dispatch pointers across four repositories recorded the lane as unknown. At those same moments the document read 32 running against a ceiling of 20. The publisher (imas_ambix/agent/lane.py) deliberately writes binding_observed: null on an SGLang lane, because the engine exposes no cumulative preemption counter. The reckon reader treats that null as a missing field, and throws away the headroom, running count and context it has just parsed. So the ceiling was never ignored; it was never received. The reader fix belongs to reckon. Whatever writes the gate file parses the lane document itself and never goes through that reader.

§9 — Make the gate unbypassable

The endpoints document publishes the engine itself, at 98dci4-gpu-0003.iter.org:18810, as endpoints[0], and the router at port 18802 only as routing_origins[0]. The engine port answers directly. A caller that takes the obvious field goes straight past the gate into the KV pool. So a gate at the router binds only callers who read the document correctly. The lead's requirement is that it bind external callers too.

First, trace every consumer of the endpoints document in this repository and in the clive launcher: which field each one dials, and whether any falls back to endpoints[0] when routing_origins is empty or unreachable. The document was republished empty during the 06:12Z outage, so the fallback case is real. Each answer cites file and line. The fix follows from the trace. The engine binds to loopback at the next relaunch, so only the router, on the same node, can reach it, and the document publishes the router as the only origin to dial. The engine port stays recorded for diagnosis under a field no consumer dials.

§8 — The root fix: more KV capacity per card

The gate protects throughput at today's capacity. Raising capacity is what lets concurrency rise while aggregate throughput holds or grows, and it is the fix to take wherever the numbers support it. Each lever below needs a serve relaunch, so they are ranked before any drain is asked for. For the installed SGLang version and the DSv4.1 profile as launched, establish four things, citing the launch line, the profile or the source for each:

  • Replication. Whether the KV cache is replicated across the four tensor-parallel ranks, what pool size each rank holds, and whether data-parallel attention is supported for this model in this version, with the pool size it would yield.
  • Dtype. The KV cache dtype in use, and whether a narrower one is supported for this model's attention without an accuracy caveat recorded by the vendor.
  • Host tier. The size of the host tier, the setting that fixes it, the host memory the serve's allocation holds against what the node has free, and whether a storage tier below host memory is available in this version.
  • Device workspace. The device memory taken by CUDA graphs, the static fraction and activation workspace at the current settings, and the pool gained by each reduction that does not change the context cap.

DP attention has a condition the projection does not show. Under DP attention each rank keeps its own radix cache. The launch uses load_balance_method=round_robin, which would scatter one agent's successive turns across four separate caches. That turns a 99% prefix hit rate into roughly a quarter of it, and costs far more than the 4× pool gains. The DP-attention experiment is admissible only with a dispatch policy that keeps a conversation on the rank holding its prefix. That is either an engine load-balance method that is prefix- or session-affine, or cache-aware routing at the router. Which of these this SGLang build supports is part of the experiment's design. Affinity is cache locality, not consumer policy. It gives no caller precedence, and a wrong choice costs a cache miss rather than a fair share, so the objection that removed the per-consumer filter does not apply to it. An agent's turns share one long, stable prefix, so hashing a session or conversation identifier to a rank should capture nearly all of the locality there is. If affinity is available, DP attention's 162 contexts beat the 8M pool's 81 without reopening the prefill-margin risk. The price is decoder bounded replay's prefill gain, and prefill is not what binds at today's contexts.

The next relaunch also sets the engine's own backstops. A bounded max_queued_requests refuses where today the engine absorbs without limit, and still binds a caller that bypasses the router. A prefix-aware schedule_policy replaces fcfs, under which one very long prefill blocks a queue of short turns. Both settings are chosen together with the router gate's wait bound, so the two layers do not refuse at different points.

For each lever, project pool tokens and the context-derived concurrency at a 98,750-token mean context, and say what the change costs. The relaunch that applies the chosen levers drains the lane to a sustained zero first, and it is authorised separately.
