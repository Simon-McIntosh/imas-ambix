Cores bind, and this sprint measured it rather than argued it

The plan asserted that cores rather than cards are the scarce resource. A bring-up attempt turned that from a claim into a measurement. Of the reservation's 30 cores, the DeepSeek serve held 8 and a co-running CPU-only job held 16 — 24 of 30 — so a serve requesting the profile's default 12 cores could not be admitted at all even with four cards sitting free. The scheduler put its start nearly three hours out.

The descending probe ladder is the practice that made this visible, and it is worth keeping:

Cores requestedScheduler verdict
12deferred, start 15:21:36
10deferred, start 15:21:36
8deferred, start 15:21:36
6immediate
4immediate

Six cores were taken, and the engine subsequently loaded 241 shards and served. The profile default would have idled four cards for three hours to no purpose. Sizing every serve from a probe rather than from arithmetic on the profile default is therefore not a precaution — it is the difference between running and not running.

The service statement

Locked to a group-sized best-effort service: it serves the storage group on spare cards with no reserved floor, so a serve may be cancelled for a training campaign, and admission limits are sized for a handful of concurrent consumers rather than a published cluster-wide wait. That matches how the node is already shared. The statement is written so widening the audience later is a card-budget change rather than a redesign.

The correction that mattered most

The sub-tree guidance described both GLM profiles as unusable stubs whose weights were unpublished. That was true when written and had become wrong in the way that actually costs something: it would have stopped someone serving a model already sitting on disk. It now records the four-card and eight-card deployments as they are, with their sizes, shared served name and differing checkpoint precision, alongside the core-budget rules it sits beside.

What can be promised

Admission control landed on the router and is recorded in full in the router record. The honest limit stands: none of it manufactures capacity. Eight cards is eight cards, and past the bound the router returns a retry directive rather than queueing without end.

The cap, demonstrated against live engines

Both releases now serve concurrently and the cap was measured against them rather than against fixtures. At a deliberately small limit of one in flight and one queued, one consumer's three requests returned 200/200/429 — the rejection carrying Retry-After 9 and arriving in 0.024 s, while its admitted requests took 9.31 s and 18.33 s. The property the cap exists for held: a second consumer was served in 0.252 s during the first's saturation, about 8.43 s before the first released its slot. The router logged eight accounting transitions across both consumer addresses, and the engine's own POST records carried only router interfaces — the first consumer's distinct address never appeared there, which is the consumer-metadata separation §1 wanted.

Reaching that point needed one more sizing pass, and it is worth recording why. The substituted checkpoint has a tighter budget than the release it replaced: at four cards it left 8.32 GiB of KV against 11.31 GiB needed, where its predecessor had reported 20.71 GiB in the same topology. Raising the memory fraction and lowering the ceiling together returned the pool to 13.9 GiB and 160,512 cached tokens — measured by the engine, not predicted.

A cap that was ours, not the scheduler's

Every profile requested a seven-day serve window while the partition reports an infinite time limit. That cap was self-imposed and would have truncated a healthy endpoint for no scheduler reason; availability here is the operator's to manage. All nine resolved profiles now request no finite cap, verified accepted by a test-only submission rather than assumed. Download limits are untouched, because transfers are bounded work.
