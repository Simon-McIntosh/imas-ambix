The knee is at 4. Up to four concurrent streams the per-stream rate is unchanged — 33.5 to 33.6 — while aggregate scales linearly to 4×, so the first four workers are free. From eight upward per-stream falls roughly as 1/concurrency while aggregate keeps rising, which is a saturated device time-slicing rather than added capacity: past the knee, concurrency buys throughput by spending latency one for one.

Aggregate had not saturated at 32 (270.7 to 350.8 for a doubling), so the ceiling is above the range measured.

Sizing guidance that follows: run a worker fleet at 4–8 concurrent. Below 4 the deployment is idle; above 8 worker wall-clock stretches without the fleet finishing sooner.

The single-stream figure is the one that disappoints against the vendor's 300–507 tok/s per-client claim, and the gap is not closed by anything available here — see §3 and the note on quantisation below.

§2 — Disposal of every claim derived from the withdrawn curve

Withdrawing a measurement and leaving the conclusions drawn from it standing is a distinct error, and it survives precisely because a conclusion reads as independent judgement once it is prose. The curve above was withdrawn on 2026-09-14; the claims built on it were not, and stayed here for hours afterwards. Each is disposed of explicitly.

ClaimDisposition
“The knee is at 4” WITHDRAWN. An artefact of the two defects. The four-card serve holds 128 concurrent requests at 2,483 tok/s and 1,536 at 4,789 tok/s with no knee found.
“Run a worker fleet at 4–8 concurrent” WITHDRAWN, and it did harm. Wrong by two orders of magnitude. Sessions reading this record found sizing advice here and self-capped; 26 concurrent live workers across four projects later ran with the engine at 53% KV occupancy and zero preemptions.
“Below 4 the deployment is idle” WITHDRAWN. Derived from the same curve.
“Aggregate had not saturated at 32” Survives, understated. True, and truer than written — saturation was not reached at 1,536 either.
The roofline argument: fixed per-token cost, not bandwidth Survives. Independent of the curve, and it is what made the caching diagnosis credible rather than correlational.

No scaling correction can rescue any of the withdrawn rows. Two defects were live and neither was visible from inside the measurement: the router refused 37.4% of requests, and the engine’s prefix caching had stopped honouring reuse across turns. So the figures are not low, they are not measuring what their labels say, and anything reasoned from them needs disposal rather than adjustment.

Replacement sizing guidance, and it is not a number. Dispatch to the width your dependency graph offers and read the engine: sustained waiting above zero, KV occupancy trending up, and num_preemptions_total leaving zero. A shared budget derived live from the engine is published by imas-ambix agent lane. Per-session seat counts do not compose across a shared engine and must not be inferred from this record. Full guidance: imas_ambix/agent/AGENTS.md §3c.

§2 — WITHDRAWN AS A CHARACTERISATION: measured through a caching defect

The curve above is real but it is not a property of this model on this hardware. It was measured while the engine's prefix caching had stopped working, so every number in it carries a per-turn cost that should not have been there. Do not cite it as the deployment's capability.

The defect, counted from the serve's own log (deepseek-v4-1-flash-1270394.log) and independently confirmed by a peer session that found it first:

MeasureValue
Prefill batches with #cached-token: 01,364 of 1,464 (93.2%)
Peak KV pool occupancy0.10 — the pool was 90% empty
Cache hits by log decile[0, 15, 53, 23, 2, 1, 0, 2, 4, 0]

It is not capacity eviction. The pool never rose above a tenth, hits and misses shared the same median occupancy, and identical back-to-back requests still cached at roughly 9.8× at the end of the serve's life. Caching worked for the first third and then stopped honouring reuse across turns, while continuing to work within a turn. A prefix that hit after one second was gone after seventy-five seconds of live traffic.

The consequence is the whole throughput story: agent traffic re-prefilled on the order of 100k tokens every turn, which is a fixed per-turn cost of about 23 seconds. The fleet saw a median of 8.6 tok/s on this serve against 17.5–101.4 on the ten preceding days.

This is the answer to a question this record previously left open. The roofline argument in §2 was right that the serve was bound by fixed per-token cost rather than by bandwidth or arithmetic — 29 ms per token against a ceiling in the high hundreds. Three candidate causes were pursued from there: speculative decoding, MXFP4 dequantisation and card count. The actual cause was in the serve's own log from the first minute, and none of the three would have fixed it. The lesson is narrower than "measure before optimising": the instrument that would have found this — counting #cached-token in the log — cost one command and was never run, while three restarts were spent on hypotheses.

Attribution: most likely a defect in lmsysorg/sglang:dev-dsv41, whose V4.1 support merged to sglang main the same day this was deployed. Recorded as a suspected engine defect, not as a tuning result, and not as a property of the checkpoint.

Peer-measured capability on the same serve while it was healthy, offered as the honest curve: 44.5 tok/s at one stream, 39.7 each at four (144.8 aggregate), 37.3 each at eight (275.8 aggregate) — 84% batch efficiency to eight streams, cold prefill 8,365 tok/s, and prefix caching demonstrably working on demand at 41× on a repeated 116k-token prefix, correctly invalidated by a single changed character.

§3 — DSpark is not viable on this topology

Speculative decoding was enabled, measured, and withdrawn. It never produced a throughput number, because it could not start: DSpark wants a ~12 GiB per-card workspace on top of weights, host-table structures, KV pool and the TensorRT-LLM autotuner.

KV poolOutcome with DSpark
14,999,808OOM at startup — asked 12.00 GiB, 1.3 GiB free
3,485,952 (smallest load-proven)OOM at startup — asked 12.00 GiB, 7.6 GiB free

Shrinking the pool cannot close the remaining 4.4 GiB, because the pool costs roughly 16 GiB of fixed base plus only ~1.6 KiB per token — there is almost nothing left to sell. This is consistent with prior experience on V4-Flash, where DSpark did not change throughput materially; here it cannot run at all. Do not re-enable without more cards, or a checkpoint whose experts are small enough to leave the Engram tables in HBM.

Pool sizing — starting is not the test

The most expensive lesson of the session. A 20,000,000-token pool started cleanly, reported its pool, answered single-stream requests and showed 14.64 GiB free per card — then died under a real request with SIGKILL 137, FlashInfer's MXFP4 fused-MoE workspace asking 7.52 GiB against 6.91 GiB free. That workspace is allocated lazily at generation, so free-memory-after-startup is not the margin; it is the margin before the largest allocation the serve will ever make.

A ladder that tested whether the engine printed max_total_num_tokens therefore recorded a pass for a configuration that could not carry load, and the failure looked healthy until something used it. Every pool figure here is now load-tested.

Stability

Ten-minute soak against job 1270323 after the 32-way sweep: 10 of 10 health probes clean on both the serve and the router, live generations returning throughout, zero OOM events, uptime 22 minutes. The one traceback in the serve log is the registry publish step failing on a missing click — an environment defect fixed separately, not a serve fault.

Withdrawn: W4A16 requantisation

Recorded because the reasoning was wrong and the next reader should not repeat it. W4A16 was proposed on the grounds that it would shrink the routed experts enough to bring the Engram tables back into HBM within four cards. The experts are already four-bit — MXFP4, 259.5 GiB for ~545B parameters — so a four-bit requantisation frees nothing and the memory argument does not exist. The premise came from carrying across the GLM-5.3 precedent, where W4A8 replaced an FP8 release and genuinely halved it.

What survives is a narrower, untested claim: that Marlin-class INT4 kernels might beat FlashInfer's MXFP4 dequantisation path on SM90. No V4.1 build in that format exists, and the V4.1 model class carries no reference to compressed-tensors, AWQ, GPTQ or Marlin, so the loader probably would not accept one. The cheap version of the same experiment is to try a different moe_runner_backend, which costs one restart and no requantisation.
