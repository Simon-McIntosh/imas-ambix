This bears directly on the planned Shafranov correction. A Shafranov shift is a radial displacement of the flux axis outboard of the current centroid, so correcting it moves the origin in R — the component that is already the stronger of the two. It cannot by construction address a vertical localisation deficit. The premise therefore needs testing in physical units before the correction is implemented, with one caveat: these are skill scores against a train-mean baseline, not errors, and per-quantity baseline variance differs, so the asymmetry must be confirmed in metres rather than inferred from skill alone.

First, the radial error dominates the vertical — 8.7 cm against 4.1 cm — the opposite of what the per-quantity skill implied. That apparent vertical deficit was a baseline-variance artifact: the train-mean baseline's own vertical spread is only [−0.005, +0.006] m, so any vertical error scores harshly against it. Skill anisotropy is not error anisotropy.

Second, and decisively, the sign is opposite to the assumed mechanism. The Shafranov premise holds that the current centroid sits inboard of the flux axis, which predicts an inboard read and therefore an outboard correction. The read axis in fact sits outboard of the referee by 8.7 cm. Displacing the origin further outboard would worsen it. An Ip-anchored moment centroid lying outboard of the EFIT flux axis is itself contrary to Shafranov physics, which points at the centroid fit rather than at the harmonic read.

The LCFS rays corroborate a single mechanism rather than two. Signed medians across 0–315° are −0.129, −0.106, −0.103, +0.104, +0.082, +0.088, −0.181, −0.126 m: the read undershoots on the outboard-upper side and overshoots inboard, which is what ray-casting from an origin displaced outboard produces. Matched X-point offsets are small by comparison (radial −0.021 and −0.011 m, vertical +0.013 and −0.016 m), so the boundary error is carried by the origin, not by the null localisation.
