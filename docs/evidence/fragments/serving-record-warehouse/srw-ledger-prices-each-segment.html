Each stretch of a ledger period is priced at its own recorded median

Node srw-ledger-prices-each-segment · date 2026-10-15 · worktree imas-ambix-0405601943d2/srw-ledger-prices-each-segment

The ledger row of a period was priced at the one open-market rate the price table happened to carry when the panel rendered, so a month that spanned several provider price changes was billed entirely at whichever rate was current at the read. The figure moved with the render time rather than with the traffic it measured. A period is now split at every instant the recorded endpoint listing for the served slug changed, and each stretch's own token total is priced at the provider median in force at that stretch's start.

What changed

  • imas_ambix/agent/watch.py gains the segment walk: _endpoint_listings (the change instants), _endpoints_in_median (how many endpoints the median was taken over, read through provider_prices' own precision floor rather than a second copy of the rule), _segment_bounds, _price_walk and PriceWalk. Each segment's tokens come from partitioned_total over its own bounds, so the split reuses the partition the query layer already defines.
  • The three pricing policies are now distinguishable in the row and in JSON: price_segments, price_policy and price_history_from ride beside cost, and the label names the policy, the endpoint count in the median, the date the history begins, a carried-back rate, a headline fallback and a segmentation that declined.
  • watch's refresh call now resolves the served model against the table already on disk, so the endpoints listing it fetches is the one for the slug this render prices, and a first run with no table names no slug and defers the listing to the next refresh.
  • tests/test_agent_watch_cost.py gains eleven tests over a fixture index and a fixture history, one per behaviour above.
  • Commit d0c797ff on the node branch, feat(agent): price each ledger period segment by segment.

The refusals that were made to happen

Each guard is exercised on a period where the guard has something to refuse, and the refusal is asserted in the label or the figure:

  • Two medians. A period spanning one recorded change costs the first segment's tokens at the first median plus the second segment's at the second, and the total is asserted not to equal the period at either rate applied to all of it.
  • A narrow endpoint. An fp4 endpoint three orders of magnitude below the wide ones moves neither the figure nor the count: the label names three endpoints for a four-endpoint listing.
  • An unrecorded segment. A stretch with no reading of its own yields a null total, and the period is then priced in one segment at the median in force at its end with segmentation not possible in the label — rather than summing the part that was measured.
  • A carried-back rate. A stretch before the history begins is priced at the first observation's median and the label says the rate was carried back, so the figure is not read as an observation of that stretch.
  • A listing with no surviving endpoint. Every endpoint below the headline, the median is taken over nothing, and the row falls back to the headline with the count of segments that fell back named.
  • A scheduled row. A time-of-day schedule cuts the period at every hour boundary — six segments against one for the same period under an unscheduled row, with the two figures equal, so the schedule is shown to move the split and not the arithmetic.

The live render, and the one condition it could not meet

One imas-ambix agent watch --json render against the live index and the live serve's receipts completed on the live record at 10:18:42Z in 2 m 33 s. The month row carried price_segments: 1 and price_policy: "headline rate", with cost: 687.593197353935 over 59,066,156,008 prompt and 406,343,459 generation tokens.

Both the segmented figure and the one-segment headline figure for that month were then computed in one process from a single read of the month's tokens. That single read is load-bearing: the serve keeps writing receipts, so two successive reads of the same month disagree by about 0.006 USD, and a comparison taken over two reads would have measured the serve's write rate rather than the pricing policy. The two figures are equal -- 687.668446434272 either way -- because the live price history holds no endpoints row at all, so the walk takes the one-segment headline path and there is nothing for a split to differ over. The comparison becomes informative at the first render that follows a refresh which actually fetched a listing.

That condition is the one the node could not satisfy inside its fence. The live history /work/projects/imas_gpu/agents/openrouter-price-history.jsonl does not exist. The refresh that would create it early-returned, which it is designed to do: the table was fetched at 09:35:46Z and REFRESH_AGE is 3600 s, so a refresh that fetches is possible only from 10:35:46Z — four minutes and forty-five seconds past this node's 10:31:01Z deadline. Waiting past the fence was not an option, and the resource was not faked: backdating the table's own at field would have misdated a real fetch, and writing a listing row into the history by hand would have put a claimed observation in the record that no fetch ever made. What was verified instead is the naming that fetch depends on. Against the live table on disk, the served model deepseek-v4.1-flash resolves to the slug deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash, and the endpoints URL the refresh will request for it is https://openrouter.ai/api/v1/models/deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash/endpoints. The refresh call itself reported refreshed: false with the table at 2111 s of age — the early return, not an error (error: None).
