Spatiotemporal Tokenizer & World-Model Survey

Authored 2026-05-29 · Research doc · Informs: plasma-decoder-finetune, tokenizer-cosmos-evaluation, tokenizer-transient-fidelity

§1 Findings Overview

The imas-ambix project currently tokenises MAST fast-visible camera frames with Open-MAGVIT2, an image (2D) LFQ tokeniser with 16× spatial compression applied independently to every frame. A decoder fine-tune trial just failed to beat the untrained baseline on per-frame rFID (~13). This survey asks whether that failure reflects a structural limitation: a frame-by-frame tokeniser that discards temporal structure cannot learn to represent plasma dynamics (ELMs, filaments, blobs, disruptions), which are inherently transient space-time phenomena.

The central finding of this survey is that the video-tokenisation literature has decisively moved past frame-by-frame approaches. Every competitive video tokeniser from 2023 onward uses causal 3D convolution or factored space-time attention to jointly compress spatial and temporal dimensions. The most important result for imas-ambix is that we tested NVIDIA Cosmos Tokeniser in its discrete-image variant only (DI16×16). The Cosmos library exposes six video variants — Continuous Video (CV) and Discrete Video (DV) at temporal compression factors of 4× or 8× — none of which have been evaluated on MAST data. These are open-weight, commercially licensed, and runnable zero-shot today on our H200 node.

A second finding concerns the metric. Per-frame rFID measures static reconstruction quality on individual frames; it is agnostic to temporal coherence. For a world model whose purpose is to predict plasma dynamics, Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) and latent-prediction accuracy are more meaningful north stars. The V-JEPA-2 architecture goes further: it eliminates pixel reconstruction entirely, predicting only in latent space, and therefore has no rFID metric at all. This is not a weakness — it is a deliberate choice informed by evidence that reconstruction objectives overfit to surface appearance rather than physical dynamics.

A third finding concerns domain transfer. All video tokenisers surveyed were pre-trained on natural-image or driving video corpora. Plasma imagery is grayscale, high-frame-rate (up to 50 kHz on fast cameras), and far from any pretraining distribution. Structured domain-gap evidence for plasma-class scientific video is sparse, but analogous evidence from medical imaging suggests that natural-image pre-training is a reasonable starting point for fine-tuning, with domain-specific fine-tuning yielding further gains. Whether zero-shot video tokenisers preserve fast-timescale transient structure in MAST rbb shots is an open empirical question — and is the primary thing the coordinator should test before committing to a tokeniser pivot.

§3 World-Model Architecture Comparison

The key structural question for imas-ambix is: where do dynamics live? In a reconstruction-based pipeline the tokeniser must encode enough temporal signal that the stage-2 autoregressive or diffusion model can predict future tokens. In a predictive (JEPA-style) pipeline dynamics are entirely in the predictor head; the encoder can be purely spatial and dynamics learning is explicit rather than implicit. Key structural observation: V-JEPA-2 stands apart from every other entry in this table. It has no reconstruction objective, no codebook, no pixel decoder, and therefore no rFID or rFVD metric. This is not an omission; it is the design. The model learns physical dynamics by predicting what will appear in spatiotemporally masked regions — a form of self-supervised temporal reasoning entirely in latent space. The trade-off is that V-JEPA-2 is a representation model, not a generative model: it cannot synthesise novel plasma imagery for data augmentation. It learns to understand dynamics without being able to reconstruct or hallucinate them.

§4 Metric Question: Is Per-Frame rFID the Right North Star for a Dynamics World Model?

The short answer is no — but the reasoning matters for how we interpret the failed decoder fine-tune.

What rFID measures

rFID (reconstruction FID) is the Fréchet Inception Distance computed on individual frames of a reconstructed video, averaging or aggregating over frames. It captures whether reconstructed frames look like real frames statistically — i.e., it is a per-frame image quality metric. A tokeniser with excellent rFID could produce frames that are each high-quality but temporally incoherent — flickering, with incorrect object trajectories, failing to preserve sub-frame events. This is exactly the failure mode relevant to plasma cameras: a blob or ELM might be present for 2–5 frames at 20 kHz; a tokeniser that smears or drops it will produce a fine rFID while being useless for transient detection.

What FVD measures and its own limitations

FVD (Fréchet Video Distance) computes the Fréchet distance between distributions of I3D features on generated vs. real video clips. It was designed to capture temporal coherence — "it captures the temporal coherence of content in addition to the quality of each frame" (source: FVD, 2018 workshop). However, 2024 work (arXiv:2404.12391) has shown that FVD has a content bias: it is often dominated by per-frame spatial quality and can be insensitive to temporal distortions (shuffling frames, corrupting motion). This means FVD is better than rFID for capturing dynamics but still imperfect.

What each architecture actually optimises

  • Reconstruction-based tokenisers (Open-MAGVIT2, Cosmos, LARP, OmniTokenizer): optimise rFID (image) or rFVD (video) as the primary tokeniser evaluation metric. Generation quality is measured separately with FVD on the downstream generation model.
  • Genie, iVideoGPT, NVIDIA Cosmos WFM: report FVD on video generation benchmarks (e.g., UCF-101, Kinetics) to measure the quality of the combined tokeniser + dynamics model. The tokeniser's rFVD and the WFM's generative FVD are reported separately.
  • LARP: reports rFVD (reconstruction) of 6.2–24 and generative FVD (gFVD) of 57 on UCF-101 (source: arXiv:2410.21264v1). The gap between rFVD and gFVD is the "generative ceiling" the tokeniser sets.
  • DIAMOND: does not report FVD or rFID; it reports agent game score on Atari 100k, because its purpose is dynamics-faithful simulation for RL. This is the most principled approach for our use-case: evaluate whether the world model enables downstream task performance, not whether it reconstructs pixels faithfully.
  • V-JEPA-2: reports no pixel-space metrics at all. Evaluation is classification accuracy (SSv2, ImageNet) and robot planning success rate. The rationale from the paper (source: arXiv:2506.09985v1): the objective "focuses on learning representations for predictable aspects of a scene...while ignoring unpredictable details that generative objectives emphasize." For a plasma world model that must understand whether an ELM occurred rather than reconstruct the exact pixel values, this philosophy is directly applicable.

Implications for the failed decoder fine-tune

The Open-MAGVIT2 decoder fine-tune was evaluated on rFID and failed to beat the untrained baseline (rFID ~13). This result has two interpretations that the choice of tokeniser affects differently:

  1. Reconstruction interpretation: the tokeniser has sufficient capacity to represent individual frames but fine-tuning the decoder does not generalise well to MAST imagery. Switching to a video tokeniser would provide temporal context but the rFID metric would still only capture per-frame quality.
  2. Representation interpretation: rFID may be the wrong metric entirely. A world model for plasma dynamics should be evaluated on whether it encodes predictive representations of transient events — something rFID cannot measure. The V-JEPA-2 approach sidesteps this by never computing rFID, instead asking directly whether the representation enables downstream prediction tasks.

The coordinator will record which interpretation governs the next evaluation protocol.

§5 Domain Transfer: Video Tokenisers on Scientific/Plasma Imagery

No video tokeniser surveyed has been pre-trained on or benchmarked against plasma camera footage. MAST rbb imagery has several properties that are out-of-distribution relative to all known pretraining corpora:

  • Grayscale or near-grayscale: MAST fast cameras capture visible emission, typically in a very narrow wavelength band. The rbb diagnostic (broad-band visible) may have colour, but many fast camera shots are effectively grayscale. All surveyed tokenisers are trained on 3-channel RGB video.
  • High frame rate and sub-millisecond transients: MAST fast cameras can run at 20–50 kHz. Standard video is 24–60 fps. At 20 kHz, an ELM event lasting 200 µs spans only 4 frames — comparable to the clip length of some tokenisers' temporal receptive fields. If temporal compression is 4×, that ELM would be compressed into a single temporal token or potentially aliased away entirely.
  • Low texture / unusual statistics: plasma emission images have bright filamentary structures against a dark background, very different from natural image statistics. Distribution shift between pretraining data and MAST shots is expected to be large.
  • No natural motion patterns: Most video tokenisers implicitly assume motion patterns consistent with natural scenes (camera motion, object motion). Plasma motion — radial blob propagation, rotating ELM structures — does not match these priors.

What the literature says about OOD / scientific video

The Interpreting Physics in Video World Models paper (arXiv:2602.07050, Feb 2026) found that physical variables (speed, acceleration, motion direction) are represented in intermediate layers of video encoders (V-JEPA-2, VideoMAE-v2) in a "Physics Emergence Zone" at approximately one-third depth. This is encouraging: physical dynamics may be accessible even from models pre-trained on non-scientific video, without requiring domain-specific pretraining. However, the paper evaluated on standard intuitive-physics benchmarks, not scientific or plasma imagery. Whether the physics emergence generalises to the specific dynamics of tokamak plasma (MHD instabilities, blob transport) is untested.

Evidence from the medical imaging domain (structurally analogous: grayscale, high texture contrast, large OOD gap from natural images) suggests that natural-image pre-trained models transfer imperfectly but usefully to domain-specific data, with domain-specific fine-tuning yielding further gains. The MedITok work (source: arXiv:2505.19225v1) demonstrates that a unified visual tokeniser can be fine-tuned for domain-specific medical modalities with significant quality improvements. This supports a strategy of starting from a pre-trained spatiotemporal tokeniser and fine-tuning on MAST data.

The SciVid benchmark (arXiv:2507.03578) evaluates video models on scientific tasks including medical computer vision, animal behaviour, and weather forecasting, but does not appear to include plasma or fusion imagery.

Temporal resolution mismatch: the aliasing risk

This is the most operationally serious domain-transfer concern. A Cosmos DV tokeniser with 4× temporal compression running on MAST rbb footage shot at, say, 5 kHz would produce one temporal token per 4 input frames (0.8 ms per token). An ELM crash typically develops over 100–500 µs — 0.5 to 2.5 tokens. Whether the tokeniser preserves the discriminative structure of such an event across a 4× compression boundary is unknown and cannot be inferred from natural-video benchmarks. An ablation that compares 1×, 2×, and 4× temporal compression on actual MAST shots, evaluated on transient-detection downstream tasks, would directly answer this question.

§6 Implications for imas-ambix: Strategic Options

The evidence from this survey supports three non-mutually-exclusive options, ordered from least to most disruptive. The coordinator will record the recommendation.

Option A — Test Cosmos DV/CV video tokeniser zero-shot (lowest risk, highest near-term return)

We already have the Cosmos Tokeniser library and H200 GPU allocation. The image variant (DI16×16) has been evaluated; the video variants (DV4×8×8, DV8×8×8, CV4×8×8, CV8×8×8) have not. Concretely: encode a subset of MAST rbb shots using DV4×8×8 (4× temporal, 8× spatial, ~64K vocab, FSQ quantisation), measure rFVD (not rFID), and run the same qualitative transient-preservation check used in tokenizer-transient-fidelity. This is a ~1-day experiment requiring no new training. Key question: does 4× temporal compression preserve ELM and blob structure at the frame rates available in rbb shots?

Risk: Cosmos was trained on natural/driving/robotics video; domain gap to plasma imagery is large. Zero-shot performance may be poor on reconstruction quality even if temporal structure is preserved. But a failed zero-shot test is diagnostic: it distinguishes "temporal compression helps but domain gap must be closed" from "temporal compression hurts regardless".

Option B — Evaluate MAGVIT-v2-style causal 3D tokenisation via an open reproduction (medium complexity)

The original MAGVIT-v2 weights are closed. However, community reproductions exist: the asymmetric MAGVIT-v2 implementation (GitHub bornfly-detachment/asymmetric_magvitv2, HuggingFace 4z and 16z models) claims to support joint image-video encoding with 4× temporal compression and the same LFQ quantisation design as MAGVIT-v2, outperforming all open-source models on FID and FVD. If weights are accessible, this would test the LFQ+3D-causal design — the closest structural upgrade from our current Open-MAGVIT2 2D model — while staying within the same quantisation family (LFQ, 2^18 vocab). The risk: these are community reproductions; training quality, licence, and exact specifications are less well-documented than Cosmos.

Option C — JEPA-style latent-predictive architecture (side-steps reconstruction entirely)

V-JEPA-2 (Meta, June 2025) learns dynamics by predicting masked spatiotemporal regions in latent space with no pixel reconstruction and no discrete tokens. If the goal is a world model that encodes plasma dynamics rather than one that reconstructs plasma imagery, this architecture eliminates rFID as a metric entirely — it is simply not part of the objective. The practical implication is that we would train (or fine-tune) a V-JEPA-2-style model on MAST shots, then evaluate on downstream prediction tasks (ELM onset classification, blob detection from latent features) rather than pixel reconstruction. This is the most principled approach for dynamics capture but requires:

  • A clear downstream evaluation task (to replace rFID as the training signal).
  • Willingness to abandon pixel-generative capabilities (no synthesised plasma imagery for data augmentation).
  • Access to V-JEPA-2 pre-trained weights for fine-tuning, or sufficient MAST data volume to train from scratch.

Option D — Fine-tune an existing spatiotemporal tokeniser on MAST data (medium-high complexity)

Any open-weight video tokeniser (Cosmos CV/DV, VidTok, LARP) can be fine-tuned on MAST rbb shots to close the domain gap. The MedITok precedent suggests this is viable. The critical design question is the training objective: fine-tuning on rFVD (video reconstruction) would improve frame fidelity but may not directly improve dynamics encoding. A fine-tune that adds a temporal-consistency or transient-detection auxiliary loss would be more targeted. This option requires GPU training time on the H200 node and is more complex than Option A.

Cross-cutting recommendation note

Options A through D are not mutually exclusive. The natural order is A (zero-shot evaluation, diagnostic) before committing to any of B, C, or D. If Option A reveals that Cosmos DV preserves transient structure adequately, it becomes the default and Option D (domain fine-tune) becomes the incremental improvement path. If Option A fails at the temporal-compression boundary, the diagnostic narrows to whether the problem is domain gap (fix: Option D) or architecture (fix: Option C). The coordinator will record the recommendation.
